    1. De Morgan’s Law.

    In class, we proved one half of DeMorgan’s Law: \((A \cap B)^c = A^c \cup B^c\text{.}\) Taking the same approach, it is your job to prove the other half:
    \begin{equation*} (A \cup B)^c = A^c \cap B^c \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Recall: To prove that two sets are equal, we must demonstrate mutual subset containment, i.e. for \(X=Y\text{,}\) we must have \(X \subseteq Y\) and \(Y \subseteq X\text{.}\)
    🔗
    (a)
    First, show that \((A \cup B)^c \subseteq A^c \cap B^c\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Start by letting \(x \in (A \cup B)^c\text{.}\) By definition of a complement, this means \(x \notin (A \cup B)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Arrange the remaining steps below in the correct logical sequence to complete both the proof. [You may or may not use all of the blocks, but you must use all blocks that you can.]
    🔗
    This means that \(x \) is not in either set, and we can say: \(x \notin A\) and \(x \notin B\text{.}\)

    
---


    Thus, \(x \in A^c\) and \(x \in B^c\text{,}\) which implies \(x \in A^c \cap B^c\text{.}\)

    
---


    Therefore, \((A \cup B)^c \subseteq A^c \cap B^c\text{.}\)

    
---


    This means that either \(x \notin A\) or \(x \notin B\text{.}\)

     #distractor
---


    Thus, \(x \in A^c\) or \(x \in B^c\text{,}\) which implies \(x \in A^c \cup B^c\text{.}\)

     #distractor
    🔗
    (b)
    Next, show the reverse containment:
    \begin{equation*} A^c \cap B^c \subseteq (A \cup B)^c\text{.} \end{equation*}
    Start by letting \(y \in A^c \cap B^c\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Arrange the remaining steps below in the correct logical sequence to complete the rest of the proof. [You may or may not use all of the blocks, but you must use all blocks that you can.]
    🔗
    By definition of an intersection, \(y \in A^c\) and \(y \in B^c\text{.}\)

    
---


    This means \(y \notin A\) and \(y \notin B\text{.}\)

    
---


    Because \(y\) fails to belong to either individual set, it cannot belong to their union, implying \(y \notin (A \cup B)\text{.}\)

    
---


    Thus, \(y \in (A \cup B)^c\text{.}\)

    
---


    Therefore, \(A^c \cap B^c \subseteq (A \cup B)^c\text{.}\)

    
---


    By definition of an intersection, \(y \in A^c\) or \(y \in B^c\text{.}\)

     #distractor

    Thus, \(y \in A^c\) or \(y \in B^c\text{,}\) which implies \(y \in A^c \cup B^c\text{.}\)

    2. Probability of a Set Difference.

    During class, we established the following property: For any two events \(A\) and \(B\text{,}\)
    \begin{equation*} P(A - B) = P(A) - P(A \cap B) \end{equation*}
    Use the axioms of probability to prove this property by reordering the steps below into the correct logical sequence. [You may or may not use all of the blocks, but you must use all blocks that you can.]
    🔗
    

    Write event \(A\) as a union of two disjoint sets:
    

    
\begin{equation*}
A = (A \cap B) \cup (A \cap B^c)
\end{equation*}

    

    
---


    

    Take the probability of both sides:
    

    
\begin{equation*}
P(A) = P[(A \cap B) \cup (A \cap B^c)]
\end{equation*}

    

    
---


    

    Apply Axiom 3:
    

    
\begin{equation*}
P(A) = P(A \cap B) + P(A \cap B^c)
\end{equation*}

    

    
---


    

    Isolate the relative components on the right side:
    

    
\begin{equation*}
P(A) - P(A \cap B) = P(A \cap B^c)
\end{equation*}

    

    
---


    

    Substitute the definition of a set difference into the equation:
    

    
\begin{equation*}
P(A) - P(A \cap B) = P(A - B)
\end{equation*}

    

    
---


    

    Apply the inclusion-exclusion principle:
    

    
\begin{equation*}
P(A) = P(A \cap B) + P(A \cup B^c) - P(B)
\end{equation*}

    

     #distractor
    Hint.
    Start by visually partitioning set \(A\text{,}\) inside a Venn diagram, into two non-overlapping pieces: the piece that intersects \(B\text{,}\) and the piece that does not touch \(B\text{.}\)

    3. Challenge Problem: Continuity of Probability.

    Let \(A_1, A_2, A_3, \dots\) be a sequence of increasing events, meaning that \(A_1 \subseteq A_2 \subseteq A_3 \subseteq \dots\text{.}\) We want to prove the continuity property of probability:
    \begin{equation*} P\left(\bigcup_{i=1}^{\infty} A_i\right) = \lim_{n \to \infty} P(A_n) \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Step 1 (Given): First, we define a sequence of disjoint events \(B_i\) to isolate the increments between the nested sets:
    \begin{align*} B_1 \amp = A_1\\ B_2 \amp = A_2 - A_1\\ B_3 \amp = A_3 - A_2\\ \amp \dots\\ B_i \amp = A_i - A_{i-1} \end{align*}
    🔗
    Arrange the remaining steps below in the correct logical sequence underneath this initial construction. The next step has been labeled for you. You will use all of the provided blocks.
    🔗
    

    Step 2: Observe that for any finite \(n\text{,}\) the following are true:
    

    
\begin{equation*}
\bigcup_{i=1}^n A_i = A_n \mbox{  and  } \bigcup_{i=1}^n B_i =A_n
\end{equation*}

    

    
---


    

    Therefore, we know that:
    

    
\begin{equation*}
\bigcup_{i=1}^n A_i = \bigcup_{i=1}^n B_i\text{.}
\end{equation*}

    

    
---


    

    This equality holds true as \(n \to \infty\text{.}\) So we can also write:
    

    
\begin{equation*}
\bigcup_{i=1}^\infty A_i = \bigcup_{i=1}^\infty B_i\text{.}
\end{equation*}

    

    
---


    

    Take the probability of each side:
    

    
\begin{equation*}
P\left(\bigcup_{i=1}^{\infty} A_i\right) = P\left(\bigcup_{i=1}^{\infty} B_i\right) 
\end{equation*}

    

    
---


    

    Since the \(B_i's\) are disjoint, we can apply Axiom 3:
    

    
\begin{equation*}
P\left(\bigcup_{i=1}^{\infty} B_i\right) = \sum_{i=1}^{\infty} P(B_i)
\end{equation*}

    

    
---


    

    Express the infinite sum as the limit of its finite partial sums (by definition):
    

    
\begin{equation*}
\sum_{i=1}^{\infty} P(B_i) = \lim_{n \to \infty} \sum_{i=1}^n P(B_i)
\end{equation*}

    

    
---


    

    Applying Axiom 3 again (backwards), we have:
    

    
\begin{equation*}
\lim_{n \to \infty} \sum_{i=1}^n P(B_i) = \lim_{n \to \infty} P\left(\bigcup_{i=1}^n B_i\right) 
\end{equation*}

    

    
---


    

    We know that \(\bigcup_{i=1}^n B_i = A_n \text{.}\) Thus:
    

    
\begin{equation*}
\lim_{n \to \infty} P\left(\bigcup_{i=1}^n B_i\right) = \lim_{n \to \infty} P(A_n)
\end{equation*}

    

    
---


    

    We are done! Putting the pieces together, we have shown that:
    

    
\begin{equation*}
P\left(\bigcup_{i=1}^{\infty} A_i\right) = \lim_{n \to \infty} P(A_n)
\end{equation*}
    Hint.
    You are given the disjoint sets \(B_i\) in step 1 of the proof. How do the finite and infinite unions of the \(A_i's\) and \(B_i's\) match up? Think about this before you introduce any limits or summations.

    2. Distributive Law.

    In-class we proved the first part of the Distributive Law:
    \begin{equation*} A \cup (B \cap C)=(A \cup B) \cap (A \cup C). \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Taking the same approach, it is your job is to prove the other half! Show that the following is true:
    \begin{equation*} A \cap (B \cup C)=(A \cap B) \cup (A \cap C) \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Recall: To prove that two sets are equal, we must demonstrate mutual subset containment, i.e. for \(X=Y\text{,}\) we must have \(X \subseteq Y\) and \(Y \subseteq X\text{.}\)
