    Section 5.6 Poisson Random Variable

    By completing the problems provided below, you will gain experience using the Poisson random variable. Specifically, you will practice:

    • Using the Poisson probability distribution function to compute probabilities.

      🔗

    1. Is it Poisson?

    Which of the following random variables \(Z\) can be appropriately modeled using a Poisson distribution? Select all that apply.
    🔗
    • A university IT help desk receives support tickets at an average rate of \(4.5\) tickets per hour during weekday mornings. Let \(Z\) be the number of tickets received between 8:30AM and 11:30AM on a randomly chosen Tuesday.
    • Correct! Tickets arrive at a constant average rate over a fixed, continuous time interval, and \(Z \) counts the number of occurrences.
    • Minor typographical errors occur randomly throughout a \(500\)-page manuscript at an average rate of \(3\) typos per 5 pages. Let \(Z\) be the total number of typos found in a randomly selected \(5\)-page section.
    • Correct! A Poisson process can occur across space, just as it does across time. Here, typos occur at a constant average rate over a fixed \(5\)-page interval, and \(Z \) counts the number of occurrences.
    • Customers arrive at a family restaurant for dinner, mostly in groups of \(2\) to \(6\) people together. On average, \(18\) customers enter the restaurant per hour. Let \(Z\) be the number of individual customers who enter between \(6:00\text{ PM}\) and \(7:00\text{ PM}\text{.}\)
    • Incorrect. Because customers arrive in groups rather than individually, counts are clustered, and the independence assumption is violated. While the number of dining parties might be Poisson, the number of individual customers is not.
    • Cars pass through a highway toll plaza at an average rate of \(20\) cars per hour during late-night hours, surging to \(150\) cars per hour during morning rush hour. Let \(Z\) be the number of cars passing through during a randomly selected \(1\)-hour window of the day.
    • Incorrect. The arrival rate is not constant. It changes dramatically between late night and rush hour. A standard Poisson model requires a constant average rate \(\lambda\) over the entire observation window.
    • Radioactive particles emitted by a sample are detected by a Geiger counter independently at a constant average rate of \(3.2\) particles per minute. Let \(Z\) be the elapsed time (in minutes) until the next particle is detected.
    • Incorrect. Even though the underlying arrivals form a Poisson process, \(Z\) measures a continuous waiting time, rather than a discrete count of events.
    • A commuter passes through \(4\) independent traffic lights on the way to work, each of which is red with probability \(0.50\text{.}\) Let \(Z\) be the number of red lights the commuter hits on a morning drive.
    • Incorrect. \(Z\) is Binomial with \(n = 4\) (traffic lights) and \(p = 0.50\text{.}\) We are considering a fixed number of trials here, rather than a continuous interval of time or space.
    Hint.
    To verify if a random variable \(Z\) follows a Poisson distribution, check for these conditions:
    🔗
    • Discrete count in a continuous interval: \(Z\) counts the number of occurrences of an event within a fixed, continuous, interval of time or space.
      🔗
      🔗
    • Constant rate of Occurrence: The average rate of occurrence (\(\lambda\)) is proportional to the length/size of the interval and does not otherwise change over time or space.
      🔗
      🔗
    • Independence: Events occur independently of one another, i.e., the number of occurrences in one interval does not affect the number in any non-overlapping interval.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    2. Emergency Room Patient Intake.

    During a busy weekend shift, trauma patients arrive at an urban emergency room according to a Poisson process with a mean rate of 45 patients per hour. If the front desk nurse steps away to grab a clean stethoscope, leaving the desk unattended for two minutes, what is the probability that more than 1 patient arrives during that brief interval?
    🔗
    • \(0.7769\)
    • Incorrect. This is the probability that 1 or more patients arrive during the two-minute window (\(P(Z \ge 1)\)).
    • \(0.5578\)
    • Incorrect. This is the probability that at most 1 patient arrives during the two-minute window (\(P(Z \le 1)\)).
    • \(0.4422\)
    • Correct! First, convert the hourly arrival rate to a 2-minute arrival rate:
      \begin{equation*} \lambda = \frac{45 \text{ patients}}{60 \text{ minutes}}\times 2 = 1.5\text{.} \end{equation*}
      Then, apply the complement rule:
      \begin{equation*} P(Z \gt 1) = 1 - [P(Z = 0)+P(Z=1)] = 1 - \left[\frac{e^{-1.5}(1.5)^0 }{0!} + \frac{e^{-1.5}(1.5)^1 }{1!}\right]= 1 - 0.5578 = 0.4422 \end{equation*}
    • \(0.3347\)
    • Incorrect. This is the probability that one patient arrives (\(P(Z=1)\)) during the two-minute time window.
    • \(0.1734\)
    • Incorrect. It’s possible that you did not properly scale your rate \(\lambda\text{.}\) Double-check your calculation for converting 45 patients per hour down to a two-minute time interval.

    1. Cryptographic Hashing.

    Bitcoin mining utilizes an algorithmic process called cryptographic hashing, which structurally behaves like a sequence of millions of independent Bernoulli trials. Each time a computing node performs a single hash, it has a tiny probability of \(p = 1.15 \times 10^{-23}\) of successfully solving the current network difficulty puzzle. Solving a block rewards the miner with a subsidy of \(3.125\) Bitcoins.
    🔗
    These operations are executed by specialized Application-Specific Integrated Circuit (ASIC) computers called miners. A typical enterprise miner performs approximately \(9.3 \times 10^{13}\) hashes per second. Suppose a commercial operation deploys a farm array of \(10\) identical miners and runs them continuously for exactly one year.
    🔗

    (a)

    Determine the total cumulative number of hashes performed by the 10-miner farm array over the course of a full 365-day year.
