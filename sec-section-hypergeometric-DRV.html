    1. Discrete Distributions: The Cookie Jar Selection.

    A cookie jar currently holds \(21\) total cookies: \(13\) are chocolate-chip cookies, and \(8\) are brussels-sprout-with-turnip-chip cookies. You pick \(3\) cookies at random from the jar without looking.
    🔗
    (a)
    Let the random variable \(X\) represent the number of brussels-sprout-with-turnip-chip cookies found in your sample. This scenario is modeled using a Hypergeometric distribution. Which of the following best explains why the Hypergeometric model applies here?
    🔗
    • Because we are sampling a fixed number of items from a finite population containing two distinct categories without replacement, meaning individual trial outcomes are dependent.
    • Correct! A hypergeometric random variable tracks a fixed sample size drawn from a small finite population divided into two groups. Because cookies are eaten or set aside (sampling without replacement), the probability of drawing a specific cookie type shifts dynamically with each draw, making the choices dependent.
    • Because each draw is independent, and the probability of picking a brussels-sprout-with-turnip-chip cookie remains perfectly constant at \(\frac{8}{21}\) for all three selections.
    • Incorrect. Constant probabilities and trial independence only hold true if you put each cookie back into the jar before selecting the next one (sampling with replacement), which fits a Binomial distribution.
    • Because we are tracking the infinite number of attempts required to pull our very first edible chocolate-chip cookie.
    • Incorrect. Counting the number of independent trials required until a success occurs describes a Geometric distribution, not a Hypergeometric distribution.
    • Because the cookies are randomly distributed over a continuous geometric interval of volume or surface area.
    • Incorrect. Continuous intervals track Poisson patterns or geometric density functions, not discrete group partitions.
    🔗
    (b)
    Hint.
    Use combinations to find the ratio:
    \begin{equation*} P(X = 0) = \frac{\binom{8}{0} \times \binom{13}{3}}{\binom{21}{3}} \end{equation*}
    Find how many ways to pick 3 cookies exclusively from the 13 chocolate-chip options, and divide by the total ways to draw any 3 cookies from the jar.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    (c)
    Hint.
    To find \(P(X \le 1)\text{,}\) calculate the sum of the two disjoint cases: \(P(X = 0) + P(X = 1)\text{.}\) You found \(P(X = 0) = \frac{286}{1330}\) in the previous part. Now evaluate \(P(X = 1) = \frac{\binom{8}{1} \times \binom{13}{2}}{\binom{21}{3}}\) and combine them.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    (d)
    Compute the expected number of brussels-sprout-with-turnip-chip cookies in your sample, \(E(X)\text{,}\) and select its correct long-term interpretation.
    🔗
    • \(E(X) \approx 1.143\text{.}\) If you repeatedly sample 3 cookies from this jar over many days, you will draw an average of about 1.143 brussels-sprout cookies per sample.
    • Correct! For a hypergeometric distribution, the expected value formula is \(E(X) = n \left(\frac{r}{N}\right)\text{,}\) where \(n=3\) is the sample size, \(r=8\) is the number of target items (brussels-sprout cookies), and \(N=21\) is the total population. This yields \(3 \times \left(\frac{8}{21}\right) = \frac{24}{21} = \frac{8}{7} \approx 1.143\text{.}\)
    • \(E(X) \approx 1.857\text{.}\) If you repeatedly sample 3 cookies from this jar over many days, you will draw an average of about 1.857 brussels-sprout cookies per sample.
    • Incorrect. This calculation uses the wrong category pool in the numerator, solving for the expected number of chocolate-chip cookies instead: \(3 \times \left(\frac{13}{21}\right) \approx 1.857\text{.}\)
    • \(E(X) = 1.143\text{.}\) In any given single sample of 3 cookies, you are guaranteed to get exactly 1 full brussels-sprout cookie and a fraction of another.
    • Incorrect. The value is mathematically right, but the interpretation is wrong. Expected values represent long-run averages over repeated trials; a single discrete draw can only yield integer values (0, 1, 2, or 3 cookies), never a fractional decimal amount.
    • \(E(X) = 0.381\text{.}\) If you repeatedly sample 3 cookies from this jar over many days, you will draw an average of about 0.381 brussels-sprout cookies per sample.
    • Incorrect. This is the simple ratio of brussels-sprout cookies in the jar (\frac{8}{21} \approx 0.381). You forgot to multiply by the sample size multiplier (n = 3).
    • E(X) = 1.500. If you repeatedly sample 3 cookies from this jar over many days, you will draw an average of exactly 1.500 brussels-sprout cookies per sample.
    • Incorrect. This is a blind guess assuming the two cookie types split the sample capacity perfectly in half (3/2 = 1.5), which ignores the actual proportions of cookies in the jar.
