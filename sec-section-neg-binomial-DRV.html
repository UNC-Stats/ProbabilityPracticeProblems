    1. Is this Negative Binomial?

    Which of the following random variables can be modeled using a Negative Binomial distribution? Select all that apply.
    🔗
    • A quality control inspector tests microchips one by one from a large production line, where each chip independently has a \(4\%\) chance of being defective. Let \(X\) be the total number of chips inspected until the \(3\text{rd}\) defective chip is found.
    • Correct! Each inspection is an independent Bernoulli trial with a constant \(4\%\) probability of “success,” and we count the total trials needed to achieve a fixed target of \(r = 3\) successes (defective chips).
    • A software deployment pipeline runs automated builds that each independently succeed with probability \(0.85\text{.}\) Let \(X\) be the number of builds that occur to get five successful builds completed.
    • Correct! Each build can be considered an independent Bernoulli trial with a constant probability of “success” (the build completes), \(p=0.85\text{.}\) Builds continue until exactly five are succesful, \(r = 3\text{.}\)
    • A basketball player makes \(78\%\) of her free throws. Let \(X\) be the number of free throws she makes out of her next \(15\) attempts. You can assume that each attempted free throw is independent of all other attempts.
    • Incorrect. Here, the number of trials is fixed in advance (\(n = 15\)), and we are counting the random number of successes. This describes a Binomial random variable, not a Negative Binomial.
    • A standard deck of \(52\) playing cards is shuffled well, and cards are dealt one at a time without replacement. Let \(X\) be the number of cards dealt until the second Ace appears.
    • Incorrect. Because cards are dealt without replacement, the trials are not independent. The probability of drawing an Ace changes after each trial.
    • A student attempts a series of logic puzzles. On each attempt, the student learns from previous mistakes, so the probability of solving a puzzle increases by \(5\%\) after every failed attempt. Let \(X\) be the number of attempts needed to solve three puzzles.
    • Incorrect. The probability of success is not constant from trial to trial because the student’s success rate increases after each failure!
    • A geological survey team drills exploratory wells in a large basin where each well independently has a \(0.12\) probability of striking oil. Let \(X\) be the number of wells drilled until they strike oil for the very first time.
    • Correct! Each well drilled can be considered an independent Bernoulli trial with a constant \(12\%\) probability of “success” (striking oil). We count the number of wells drilled until oil is found (stop after first success), \(r = 1\text{.}\)
      Note: Random variable \(X\) can also be considered Geometric. The Geometric random variable is a special case of the Negative Binomial where \(r = 1\) specifically.
    Hint.
    To determine if a variable fits the geometric model, check if it satisfies the following assumptions:
    🔗
    • Binary - Are there exactly two complementary outcomes for each trial (e.g., Success vs. Failure)? Bernoulli trials!
      🔗
      🔗
    • Independent - Does the outcome of one trial have no effect on the outcome of another?
      🔗
      🔗
    • Constant Probability of Success - Does the probability (\(𝑝\)) stay constant for every single trial?
      🔗
      🔗
    • Stop After the r-th Success - Does the variable count the trials or attempts required until the r-th success occurs (with the trials stopping at that point)?
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗

    If the salesperson gets a $250 bonus for each painting agreement secured, what is the mean number of household visits required to hit a bonus target of $3,000?
    🔗
    • \(12\) houses
    • Incorrect. This is the value of \(r\text{,}\) the number of successful painting agreements required to hit $3,000 (\(\frac{3000}{250} = 12\)). You still need to calculate the expected number of total household visits required to land that many sales.
    • \(30,000\) houses
    • Incorrect. It looks like you divided the entire $3,000 revenue target by the probability parameter \(p = 0.10\text{.}\) Make sure to convert your revenue target to a target number of successful sales (\(r\)) first.
    • \(120\) houses
    • Correct! First, determine the number of target successes (\(r\)) needed to reach the financial goal:
      \begin{equation*} r = \frac{\$3000 \text{ target}}{\$250 \text{ per job}} = 12 \text{ agreements} \end{equation*}
      Next, apply the expected value function using our parameter count of \(r = 12\) and success probability \(p = 0.10\text{:}\)
      \begin{equation*} E(Y) = \frac{r}{p} = \frac{12}{0.10} = 120 \text{ houses} \end{equation*}
    • \(1.2\) houses
    • Incorrect. It looks like you multiplied the target sales number of 12 by the probability parameter \(p = 0.10\) instead of dividing by it. The expected number of trials will always be larger than the target number of successes when \(p \lt 1\text{.}\)
    • \(108\) houses
    • Incorrect. This is the number of failures before reaching the target number of successes.

    1. Alternative Definitions for Waiting-Time Distributions.

    Both the geometric and negative binomial random variables can be defined ed by tracking either the total number of trials or the total number of failures. Let us define the alternative frameworks:
    • Counting Failures:
      🔗
      • Geometric: Let \(X_G\) be the number of failures before the first success occurs.
        🔗
        🔗
      • Negative Binomial: Let \(X_{NB}\) be the number of failures before the \(r\)-th success occurs.
        🔗
        🔗
      🔗
    • Counting Total Trials:
      🔗
      • Geometric: Let \(Y_G\) be the total number of trials required to reach the first success.
        🔗
        🔗
      • Negative Binomial: Let \(Y_{NB}\) be the total number of trials required to reach the \(r\)-th success.
        🔗
        🔗
      🔗
    🔗

    (a)

    Write an algebraic equation relating the random variables \(X_G\) and \(Y_G\text{,}\) and another equation relating \(X_{NB}\) and \(Y_{NB}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    (b)

    Use your answer to part (a), properties for expected value, and what you know about \(Y_{NB}\) (counting total trials) to show that the expected value of \(X_{NB}\) (counting failures) is \(E(X_{NB})=\frac{rq}{p}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    (c)

    Use your answer to part (a), properties for variance, and what you know about \(Y_{NB}\) (counting total trials) to show that the variance for \(X_{NB}\) (counting failures) is \(V(X_{NB})=\frac{rq}{p^2}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    (d)

    Use your answer to part (a), properties for moment generating functions, and what you know about \(Y_{NB}\) (counting total trials) to show that the moment generating function for \(X_{NB}\) (counting failures) is \(M_{X_{NB}}(s)=\left(\frac{p}{1-qe^s}\right)^r\text{.}\)
