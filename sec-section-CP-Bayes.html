    1. Smart Filter.

    An AI-powered application filters incoming phone notifications into three categories that partition a student’s daily alerts: academic alerts (\(A_1\)), student club updates (\(A_2\)), and promotional spam (\(A_3\)). The probabilities for incoming notifications are:
    \begin{equation*} P(A_1) = 0.3, \quad P(A_2) = 0.1, \quad P(A_3) = 0.6 \end{equation*}
    Let \(E\) be the event that a notification triggers an immediate haptic vibration alert. The app’s filter settings yield the following conditional probabilities:
    \begin{equation*} P(E \mid A_1) = 0.3, \quad P(E \mid A_2) = 0.5, \quad P(E \mid A_3) = 0.7 \end{equation*}
    🔗
    (a)
    Hint.
    Apply the Law of Total Probability!
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    (b)
    Suppose the student’s phone vibrates, what is the probability that this specific notification came from a student club update?
    🔗
    • \(0.089\)
    • Correct! We compute \(P(A_2 \mid E)\) by applying Bayes’ Theorem:
      \begin{equation*} P(A_2 \mid E)=\frac{P(E \mid A_2)P(A_2)}{P(E)} = \frac{0.05}{0.56} \approx 0.089\text{.} \end{equation*}
    • \(0.500\)
    • Incorrect. This is the conditional probability \(P(E \mid A_2)\text{.}\) You need to compute \(P(A_2 \mid E)\text{.}\)
    • \(0.100\)
    • Incorrect. This is the prior probability \(P(A_2)\text{.}\) You need to compute \(P(A_2 \mid E)\text{.}\) [Note: Because student club alerts are less likely to vibrate than other alerts, knowing that a vibration happened should decrease the probability of \(A_2\) slightly.
    • \(0.179\)
    • Incorrect. You most likely calculated the numerator of Bayes’ Theorem incorrectly. Try \(P(E\cap A_2)=P(E \mid A_2)P(A_2)\) .
    • \(0.893\)
    • Incorrect. You most likely calculated the numerator of Bayes’ Theorem incorrectly. Try \(P(E\cap A_2)=P(E \mid A_2)P(A_2)\) .
    Hint.
    Apply Bayes’ Theorem. You calculated the denominator in part (a).

    4. Email Scanner Diagnostics.

    An automated campus server uses a machine learning algorithm to scan incoming email files. Let \(M\) be the event that a file is malicious software, and let \(T\) be the event that the scanner flags the file as a threat. The scanner’s system analytics provide the following probabilities:
    \begin{equation*} P(M) = 0.14, \quad P(T \mid M) = 0.92, \quad P(T^c \mid M^c) = 0.67 \end{equation*}
    If a randomly arriving file is flagged as a threat by the scanner, what is the probability that it is actually a malicious software file?
    🔗
    • \(0.312\)
    • Correct! Apply Bayes’ Theorem:
      \begin{equation*} P(M \mid T) = \frac{0.1288}{0.4126} \approx 0.312\text{.} \end{equation*}
    • \(0.413\)
    • Incorrect. This value is the total probability that a file gets flagged as a threat by the scanner, \(P(T)\text{.}\) You must use this number as your denominator in Bayes’ Theorem to find the conditional probability \(P(M \mid T)\text{.}\)
    • \(0.129\)
    • Incorrect. This value is the probability of the intersection \(P(M \cap T)\text{.}\) You must use this number as your numerator in Bayes’ Theorem to find the conditional probability \(P(M \mid T)\text{.}\)
    • \(0.920\)
    • Incorrect. This value is the true positive rate (conditional probability) that is given. You need to find \(P(M \mid T)\text{.}\)
    • \(0.688\)
    • Incorrect. This is \(P(M^c \mid T)\text{,}\) which is the complement of what you need to find.

    1. World Cup.

    Your favorite national football team is competing in the FIFA World Cup. Based on their qualifying record and performance metrics (compiled by sports analytics models), you assign an initial probability of \(64\%\) that they will win the entire championship.
    🔗
    Historical tournament records indicate that when a team ultimately wins the World Cup, they win their first group stage match \(71\%\) of the time. When they fail to win the championship, they win their first group stage match only \(20\%\) of the time.
    🔗
    The opening match is over, and your team has not won. What is the probability that they will still go on to win the World Cup?
    🔗
    Hint.
    If you’re not sure where to start, then try using the complement rule to find the probability that the team does not win their first match, given they win the tournament.

    2. The Weighted Collectible Die Experiment.

    A tabletop gaming store owner purchases an unverified chest containing a total of 16 identical-looking custom, collectible d20 (20-sided) gaming dice. Within the chest, 11 are authentic factory originals that are perfectly balanced and fair, landing on the maximum critical hit symbol exactly 5% of the time. The remaining 5 dice are counterfeit, weighted so that they land on the critical hit symbol with a probability of \(1/4\text{.}\)
    🔗
    (a)
    Suppose the store owner reaches into the chest, picks exactly one die completely at random, and rolls it once. What is the probability that the die lands on the critical hit symbol?
    🔗
    🔗
    (b)
    Suppose the store owner picks a die completely at random and rolls it once, and it lands on a critical hit. Given this outcome, what is the probability that the die is one of the counterfeits?
    🔗
    🔗
    (c)
    Suppose the store owner picks a die completely at random and rolls that same exact die three times in a row, and it lands on a critical hit every time. Given this consecutive streak, what is the probability that the dice is one of the counterfeits?

    3. [Review Question] Fandom Convention Analytics.

    An organizer at a multi-genre pop culture convention conducts an entry poll of attendees. The survey data reveals that 28% of the attendees are wearing apparel from an anime series. Among those wearing anime apparel, exactly 25% are also wearing merchandise from a sci-fi media franchise. Additionally, it is recorded that 35% of all surveyed convention attendees are wearing merchandise from a sci-fi media franchise.
    🔗
    (a)
    Suppose an attendee is selected at random from this convention crowd. What is the probability that the randomly selected attendee is wearing apparel from an anime series, given that they are wearing merchandise from a sci-fi media franchise?
    🔗
    🔗
    (b)
    Determine if the selection of anime apparel and sci-fi media franchise merchandise are independent, positively correlated, or negatively correlated. Justify your reasoning.
    🔗
    🔗
    (c)
    What is the probability that a randomly selected convention attendee is wearing neither anime apparel nor sci-fi media franchise merchandise?
