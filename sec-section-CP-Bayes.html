    1. Smart Filter Metrics and Bayes’ Theorem.

    An AI-powered notification app filters a student’s incoming phone pings into three mutually exclusive categories that partition their daily alerts: Academic alerts (\(A_1\)), Student Club updates (\(A_2\)), and Promotional spam (\(A_3\)). The base probabilities for an incoming notification falling into these groups are:
    \begin{equation*} P(A_1) = 0.3, \quad P(A_2) = 0.1, \quad P(A_3) = 0.6 \end{equation*}
    Let \(E\) be the event that a notification triggers an immediate haptic vibration alert. The app’s filter settings yield the following conditional probabilities:
    \begin{equation*} P(E \mid A_1) = 0.3, \quad P(E \mid A_2) = 0.5, \quad P(E \mid A_3) = 0.7 \end{equation*}
    🔗
    (a)
    Hint.
    Apply the Law of Total Probability: \(P(E) = P(E \mid A_1)P(A_1) + P(E \mid A_2)P(A_2) + P(E \mid A_3)P(A_3)\text{.}\) Multiply each conditional probability by its corresponding category weight, then add them all together.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    (b)
    Suppose the student’s phone vibrates, indicating event \(E\) has occurred. What is the updated posterior probability \(P(A_2 \mid E)\) that this specific notification came from a Student Club update?
    🔗
    • \(0.0893\)
    • Correct! By applying Bayes’ Theorem, we calculate the joint intersection of a Club update vibrating divided by the total vibration probability found in Part 1: \(\frac{P(E \mid A_2)P(A_2)}{P(E)} = \frac{0.5 \times 0.1}{0.56} = \frac{0.05}{0.56} \approx 0.0893\text{.}\)
    • \(0.5000\)
    • Incorrect. This is the forward conditional probability \(P(E \mid A_2)\text{.}\) You must look for the backward posterior probability \(P(A_2 \mid E)\) by factoring in how rare Club updates are relative to everything else.
    • \(0.1000\)
    • Incorrect. This is the prior baseline probability \(P(A_2)\) before checking if the phone vibrated. Because Club alerts are less likely to vibrate than promotional updates, knowing that a vibration happened should decrease this probability slightly.
    • \(0.1786\)
    • Incorrect. This mistake occurs if you forget to factor in the base rate, dividing \(P(E \mid A_2)\) directly by \(P(E)\) (\(0.5 / 0.56\)). Remember to multiply the numerator by the prior probability weight \(P(A_2) = 0.1\text{.}\)
    Hint.
    Apply Bayes’ Theorem:
    \begin{equation*} P(A_2 \mid E) = \frac{P(E \mid A_2)P(A_2)}{P(E)} \end{equation*}
    Substitute your individual target segment count into the numerator and utilize the total probability value calculated in Part 1 as your denominator.

    3. Workplace Drug Testing and Conditional Probability.

      An logistics company uses a rapid oral fluid screening test to determine whether machine operators have controlled substances in their system. The rapid screening kit is not totally reliable:
      • \(5\%\) of employees who have not consumed a controlled substance give a false-positive reading (the test indicates drug presence).
        🔗
        🔗
      • \(10\%\) of employees who have consumed a controlled substance give a false-negative reading (the test indicates no drug presence).
        🔗
        🔗
      Suppose that, in fact, \(17\%\) of the applicant pool has consumed a controlled substance, and the safety manager tests an employee at random.
      🔗
      Match each conditional probability question on the left with its correct calculated value on the right. Note that there are extra numbers on the right that do not match either scenario!
      🔗
    • Find the probability that the employee has consumed the substance, given that the test reading is positive.
    • \(0.7866\)
    • Find the probability that the employee has not consumed the substance, given that the test reading is negative.
    • \(0.9789\)
    • \(0.1945\)
    • \(0.8055\)
    • \(0.9000\)
    Hint.
    Let \(D\) be the event the drug is present, and \(T\) be a positive test. Organize your components first: \(P(D) = 0.17\text{,}\) \(P(T \mid D^c) = 0.05\text{,}\) and \(P(T^c \mid D) = 0.10\text{.}\) Use the Law of Total Probability to find the baseline denominators \(P(T)\) and \(P(T^c)\) before finalizing your Bayes’ Theorem matrices.

    4. World Cup Tournament Odds and Bayes’ Theorem.

    Your favorite national football team is competing in the FIFA World Cup. Based on their qualifying record and performance metrics (compiled by sports analytics models), you assign an initial probability of \(64\%\) that they will win the entire championship.
    🔗
    Historical tournament records indicate that when a team ultimately wins the World Cup, they win their first group stage match \(71\%\) of the time. When they fail to win the championship, they win their first group stage match only \(20\%\) of the time.
    🔗
    The opening match is over, and your favorite team has lost. Assuming group stage matches cannot end in a draw for this scenario, what is the probability that they will still go on to win the World Cup?
    🔗
    • \(0.392\)
    • Correct! Let \(W\) be winning the tournament and \(L\) be losing the first match. We find the conditional complements: \(P(W^c) = 0.36\text{,}\) \(P(L \mid W) = 1 - 0.71 = 0.29\text{,}\) and \(P(L \mid W^c) = 1 - 0.20 = 0.80\text{.}\) Using the Law of Total Probability, the total probability of losing the first match is \(P(L) = (0.29)(0.64) + (0.80)(0.36) = 0.1856 + 0.2880 = 0.4736\text{.}\) Applying Bayes’ Theorem yields \(P(W \mid L) = \frac{0.1856}{0.4736} \approx 0.3919\text{,}\) which rounds to \(0.392\text{.}\)
    • \(0.473\)
    • Incorrect. This value represents the total probability that the team loses their opening match, \(P(L) = 0.4736\text{.}\) You must use this value as the denominator in Bayes’ Theorem rather than the final conditional answer.
    • \(0.512\)
    • Incorrect. This distractor corresponds to an arithmetic tracking error where the forward probability of winning the first match is substituted instead of its complementary loss.
    • \(0.556\)
    • Incorrect. This occurs if you fail to calculate the total probability space properly, using un-weighted conditional segments in your denominator.
    • \(0.640\)
    • Incorrect. This is your initial prior probability \(P(W)\text{.}\) Losing the opening game provides new, negative evidence, meaning your updated tournament outlook must be strictly lower than \(0.640\text{.}\)
    Hint.
    You are looking for the conditional probability \(P(\text{Win World Cup} \mid \text{Lost First Match})\text{.}\) First, calculate the probability of a team losing their first match given they win the tournament (\(1 - 0.71 = 0.29\)) and given they lose the tournament (\(1 - 0.20 = 0.80\)). Use these to calculate the total probability of a first-match loss before evaluating Bayes’ Theorem.

    5. Smart Filter Diagnostics and Bayes’ Theorem.

    An automated campus server uses a machine learning filter to scan incoming files. Let \(A\) be the event that a file is malicious software, and let \(B\) be the event that the filter flags the file as a threat. The system analytics provide the following base probabilities:
    \begin{equation*} P(A) = 0.44, \quad P(B \mid A) = 0.03, \quad P(B^c \mid A^c) = 0.06 \end{equation*}
    If a randomly arriving file is flagged as a threat by the filter, what is the posterior probability \(P(A \mid B)\) that it is actually a malicious software file?
    🔗
    • \(0.0245\)
    • Correct! First, find the complements: \(P(A^c) = 1 - 0.44 = 0.56\) and \(P(B \mid A^c) = 1 - 0.06 = 0.94\text{.}\) Next, apply the Law of Total Probability to find the denominator: \(P(B) = (0.03)(0.44) + (0.94)(0.56) = 0.0132 + 0.5264 = 0.5396\text{.}\) Finally, apply Bayes’ Theorem: \(P(A \mid B) = \frac{0.0132}{0.5396} \approx 0.0245\text{.}\)
    • \(0.5396\)
    • Incorrect. This value is the total probability that a file gets flagged by the filter, \(P(B)\text{.}\) You must use this number as your denominator in Bayes’ Theorem to find the conditional probability \(P(A \mid B)\text{.}\)
    • \(0.0132\)
    • Incorrect. This value is the joint probability of the intersection \(P(A \cap B) = P(B \mid A)P(A) = 0.0132\text{.}\) Remember to divide this numerator by the total probability space \(P(B)\text{.}\)
    • \(0.9400\)
    • Incorrect. This value is the false positive rate condition, \(P(B \mid A^c) = 1 - P(B^c \mid A^c) = 0.94\text{.}\) This represents the probability of a safe file being flagged, not a flagged file being malicious.
    • \(0.0236\)
    • Incorrect. This result stems from a minor arithmetic carrying error when computing the intersection components. Double-check your decimals during the summation phase.
    Hint.
    You need to set up Bayes’ Theorem: \(P(A \mid B) = \frac{P(B \mid A)P(A)}{P(B)}\text{.}\) Before calculating, look closely at your given values: you are given \(P(B^c \mid A^c) = 0.06\text{,}\) which means its conditional complement is \(P(B \mid A^c) = 1 - 0.06 = 0.94\text{.}\) Use that to find \(P(B)\) first.

    A tabletop gaming store owner purchases an unverified chest containing a total of 16 identical-looking custom, collectible d20 (20-sided) gaming dice. Within the chest, 11 are authentic factory originals that are perfectly balanced and fair, landing on the maximum critical hit symbol exactly 5% of the time. The remaining 5 dice are counterfeit, weighted so that they land on the critical hit symbol with a probability of \(1/4\text{.}\)
    🔗
    (a)
    Suppose the store owner reaches into the chest, picks exactly one die completely at random, and rolls it once. What is the probability that the die lands on the critical hit symbol?
    🔗
    🔗
    (b)
    Suppose the store owner picks a die completely at random and rolls it once, and it lands on a critical hit. Given this outcome, what is the probability that the dice is one of the counterfeits?
    🔗
    🔗
    (c)
    Suppose the store owner picks a die completely at random and rolls that same exact die three times in a row, and it lands on a critical hit every time. Given this consecutive streak, what is the probability that the dice is one of the counterfeits?
