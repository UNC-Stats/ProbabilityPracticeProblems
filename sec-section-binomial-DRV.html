    1. Is this Binomial? [Part 1].

    In a recent year, the nationwide average score on the National Academic Assessment (NAA) was 1050. Suppose that exactly 54% of all high school graduates took this exam, and that female students comprise 51% of all high school graduates nationwide.
    🔗
    A random sample of 120 high school graduates is selected from across the country. Below you are presented with multiple random variables related to this scenario. Select all that can be be considered Binomial/can be modeled with a Binomial distribution.
    🔗
    • The number of students in the sample who took the NAA.
    • Correct! This counts independent "successes" (taking the test) out of a fixed number of trials. Here, \(n = 120\) and \(p = 0.54\text{.}\)
    • The individual exam scores of the 120 students in the sample.
    • Incorrect. These are numerical values measured on a continuous scale, not a count of binary successes/failures.
    • The number of students in the sample who scored above average on the NAA.
    • Correct! Each student either scored above average or they did not (binary outcomes). While \(n = 120\text{,}\) the exact value of \(p\) is unknown based on the given information.
    • The amount of time required by each student in the sample to complete the NAA.
    • Incorrect. Time is a continuous quantitative random variable, not a discrete count of binary events.
    • The number of female high school graduates in the sample.
    • Correct! This is a classic binomial counting scenario with a fixed sample size. Each student is either female or not (binary outcomes). Here, \(n = 120\) and \(p = 0.51\text{.}\)
    Hint.
    To determine if a variable fits the binomial model, check if it satisfies the following assumptions:
    🔗
    • Binary - Are there exactly two complementary outcomes for each trial (e.g., Success vs. Failure)? Bernoulli trials!
      🔗
      🔗
    • Independent - Does the outcome of one trial have no effect on the outcome of another?
      🔗
      🔗
    • Fixed Number of Trials - Is the sample size (\(n\)) established before counting begins?
      🔗
      🔗
    • Constant Probability of Success - Does the probability (\(p\)) stay constant for every single trial?
      🔗
      🔗

    2. Is this Binomial? [Part 2].

    An electronics logistics warehouse receives a batch of 80 smart sensors, exactly 8 of which have a calibration defect. A quality control engineer plans to audit the shipment under various testing protocols.
    🔗
    Select all of the following random variables that can be modeled using a Binomial distribution (or are well-approximated by one).
    🔗
    • The engineer randomly selects 5 sensors with replacement (testing and returning each one before picking the next), and records the number of defective sensors found.
    • Correct! Because sampling is done with replacement, the trials are independent and the probability of finding a defect stays perfectly constant at \(p = 8/80 = 0.10\) across a fixed \(n = 5\) trials.
    • The engineer randomly selects 5 sensors without replacement from the batch of 80, and records the number of defective sensors found.
    • Incorrect. Because the batch is small (\(N = 80\)) and sampling is done without replacement, the trials are dependent—the probability of a defect changes with each pull. This describes a Hypergeometric distribution, not a Binomial one.
    • The engineer tests sensors one by one with replacement and records the total number of sensors they must test until they discover their very first defective unit.
    • Incorrect. This scenario counts the number of trials required to achieve the first success, which defines a Geometric distribution, not a Binomial one.
    • The warehouse receives a massive new fleet of 15,000 components from an assembly line where 2% are known to be defective. The engineer randomly selects 50 components without replacement and counts the defective units.
    • Correct! While sampling is technically without replacement, the sample size (\(n = 50\)) is less than 10% of the massive population (\(N = 15,000\)). The independence assumption holds, making the Binomial approximation to the hypergeometric perfectly appropriate here (\(n=50, p=0.02\)).
    • The engineer randomly selects 5 sensors with replacement and records the total weight of the selected units.
    • Incorrect. Total weight is a continuous quantitative metric, not a discrete count of binary success events.
