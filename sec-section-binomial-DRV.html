    1. Binomial Distributions: Colorado Energy Conservation Audits.

    Many utility companies promote environmental sustainability and energy conservation by offering discount rates to consumers who keep their monthly energy usage below certain established subsidy standards. A recent state environmental report notes that 35% of residential energy consumers in Colorado have reduced their home electricity usage sufficiently to qualify for these discounted rates.
    🔗
    Suppose an energy inspector randomly selects a sample of 5 residential subscribers from Denver, Colorado. Let \(X\) denote the number of chosen subscribers who qualify for the favorable discounted rates.
    🔗
    (a)
    🔗
    (b)
    🔗
    (c)
    Calculate the expected number and the variance of qualifying households within a random sample of \(5\) subscribers. Which option correctly identifies these values and delivers the most accurate interpretation of their meaning?
    🔗
    • \(E(X) = 3.50\) households, \(\text{Var}(X) = 1.05\text{.}\) This indicates that a clear majority of the sampled households will reliably qualify for the subsidy standard during most audit cycles.
    • Incorrect. This configuration uses a success probability of \(p = 0.70\) from the old scenario. With Colorado’s realistic baseline rate of 35%, the mean must shift lower, indicating that qualifying is the minority outcome state rather than the majority default.
    • \(E(X) = 1.75\) households, \(\text{Var}(X) = 1.14\text{.}\) This indicates that while you expect nearly 2 households to qualify on average, a variance close to the mean reveals high relative sample-to-sample volatility.
    • Correct! For any valid discrete random variable following a standard Binomial distribution, we evaluate the summary moments using algebraic parameter shortcuts:
      • Expected Value (Mean): \(E(X) = n \cdot p = 5 \cdot 0.35 = 1.75\) households.
      • Variance: \(\text{Var}(X) = n \cdot p \cdot (1 - p) = 5 \cdot 0.35 \cdot 0.65 = 1.1375 \approx 1.14\text{.}\)
      Strategic Interpretation: In a small sample of 5, an expected value of 1.75 paired with a variance of 1.14 tells auditors to expect massive cluster fluctuations. Because the variance is high relative to the mean, drawing a sample with absolutely 0 qualifiers or a sample where 4 out of 5 qualify are both standard operational occurrences, rather than highly unusual anomalies.
    • \(E(X) = 1.75\) households, \(\text{Var}(X) = 1.75\text{.}\) This indicates that the system behaves as a standard Poisson stream where the arrival count and dispersion are perfectly locked.
    • Incorrect. While a Poisson distribution features an identical mean and variance (\(\mu = \sigma^2\)), a Binomial distribution’s variance formula incorporates a regular dampening multiplier for failures, \((1 - p)\text{.}\) Because \(1 - 0.35 = 0.65 \lt 1\text{,}\) the variance must be strictly smaller than the mean: \(1.14 \lt 1.75\text{.}\)
    • \(E(X) = 2.50\) households, \(\text{Var}(X) = 1.25\text{.}\) This indicates a perfectly symmetric, unskewed coin-tossing model where succeeding or failing to qualify are completely equally likely.
    • Incorrect. This matches a uniform or fair-coin system where \(p = 0.50\text{.}\) Because Colorado’s actual conservation rate is lower (\(p = 0.35\)), the center of gravity shifts left and the distribution is right-skewed.

    2. Binomial Distributions: Redundant Engineering Systems in Gaming Tech.

    During a championship sci-fi esports tournament, a media production company deploys an automated broadcast drone array to hover over the arena. To ensure stability, the drone’s primary stabilization subsystem features a redundant bank of four identical hover-thrusters. Each individual thruster operates completely independently of the others.
    🔗
    Due to the extreme heat during the live event match, each individual thruster has a probability of \(p = 0.20\) of overheating and failing before the 1,000-second match countdown timer finishes. Crucially, the stabilization subsystem is designed with fail-safes: it will keep the drone airborne if any two of the four thrusters remain operational.
    🔗
    (a)
    🔗
    (b)
    Calculate the probability that the drone stabilization subsystem successfully operates longer than the 1,000-second tournament window.
    🔗
    • A. \(0.1536\)
    • Incorrect. This is the isolated point probability that exactly two components survive (\(P(X = 2)\)). The drone subsystem remains operational if \(two, three, \text{ or } four\) components function cleanly, so you must find the cumulative area for the entire interval.
    • B. \(0.0272\)
    • Incorrect. This is the catastrophic failure rate (\(P(X \lt 2)\)), which counts the scenarios where either 0 or only 1 thruster stays online. Subtract this value from total certainty (1.00) to isolate the successful operating space.
    • C. \(0.9728\)
    • Correct! Because we have a fixed set of independent components (\(n = 4\)) where each has a constant binary success rate of remaining online (\(p_{\text{success}} = 1 - 0.20 = 0.80\)), the random count \(X\) follows a standard Binomial profile: \(X \sim \text{Binomial}(n = 4, p = 0.80)\text{.}\)
      The system successfully operates if \(X \ge 2\text{.}\) Applying the Complement Rule makes this check highly efficient:
      \begin{equation*} P(X \ge 2) = 1 - P(X \lt 2) = 1 - \left[ P(X = 0) + P(X = 1) \right] \end{equation*}
      Evaluating the individual failure states using the Binomial PMF:
      • Exactly 0 components survive: \(\binom{4}{0} (0.80)^0 (0.20)^4 = 1 \cdot 1 \cdot 0.0016 = 0.0016\)
      • Exactly 1 component survives: \(\binom{4}{1} (0.80)^1 (0.20)^3 = 4 \cdot 0.80 \cdot 0.0080 = 0.0256\)
      Summing these lower tail blocks and subtracting from 1 yields: \(1 - (0.0016 + 0.0256) = 1 - 0.0272 = 0.9728\text{.}\) The cluster completes its run with a 97.28% reliability rate.
    • D. \(0.8192\)
    • Incorrect. This represents the probability that the system encounters at most one failure, but the calculation step forgot to include the perfect scenario where absolutely zero thrusters overheat (\(P(X = 4)\)).
    • E. \(0.4096\)
    • Incorrect. This looks like the isolated point probability that exactly three components survive (\(P(X = 3)\)). Make sure to gather the full structural interval area across all operating states.
    🔗
    (c)
    Calculate the expected number and the variance of surviving thrusters in this 4-element array block. Which option correctly identifies these values and maps out the true risk behavior of the tech array?
    🔗
    • \(E(X) = 0.80\) thrusters, \(\text{Var}(X) = 0.64\text{.}\) This indicates that under normal conditions, almost all thrusters are expected to burn out immediately during the countdown launch sequence.
    • Incorrect. This option swaps the probability parameters, using the failure rate of 0.20 as the target success probability. Because each thruster has a dominant 80% chance of staying online, the expected survival count must be high.
    • \(E(X) = 3.20\) thrusters, \(\text{Var}(X) = 0.64\text{.}\) This confirms that while the vast majority of parts stay operational on average, a low variance keeps the cluster highly clustered around safe operating levels.
    • Correct! For any valid discrete random variable following a standard Binomial distribution, we evaluate the summary moments using algebraic parameter shortcuts based on our success parameters (\(n = 4\text{,}\) \(p = 0.80\)):
      • Expected Value (Mean): \(E(X) = n \cdot p = 4 \cdot 0.80 = 3.20\) operational thrusters.
      • Variance: \(\text{Var}(X) = n \cdot p \cdot (1 - p) = 4 \cdot 0.80 \cdot 0.20 = 0.64\text{.}\)
      Operational Interpretation: An expected value of 3.20 proves that the system is engineered defensively. Since you only need 2 thrusters to survive, a mean of 3.20 paired with a very tight variance of 0.64 ensures that the random outcome is strongly squashed against the upper boundary, making a catastrophic drop down to 0 or 1 active thrusters an exceedingly rare edge case anomaly.
    • \(E(X) = 3.20\) thrusters, \(\text{Var}(X) = 3.20\text{.}\) This implies the system’s failure patterns behave like a standard continuous Poisson decay field.
    • Incorrect. While a Poisson system features a locked mean and variance, a Binomial distribution dampens its variance by multiplying by the failure weight, \((1-p)\text{.}\) Since \(0.20 \lt 1\text{,}\) the variance must be strictly smaller than the mean: \(0.64 \lt 3.20\text{.}\)
    • \(E(X) = 2.00\) thrusters, \(\text{Var}(X) = 1.00\text{.}\) This models a perfectly balanced system where turning on or burning out are entirely equally likely.
    • Incorrect. This matches a fair coin model where \(p = 0.50\text{.}\) Because the components are high-grade and heavily biased toward survival (\(p = 0.80\)), the center of mass sits much further right.
