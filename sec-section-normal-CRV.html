    2. Continuous Distributions: Normal Distribution Exam Times.

    We previously modeled the amount of time that it takes a MATH 350 student to complete a probability exam as a continuous uniform random variable. A more realistic and accurate model would be the normal distribution.
    🔗
    Assuming exam completion times follow a normal distirbution with a mean of 40 minutes and a standard deviation of 5 minutes, compute the following probabilities.
    🔗
    (a)
    Find the probability that a randomly selected student requires more than 51 minutes to complete the exam (\(P(X \gt 51)\)).
    🔗
    • \(0.9861\)
    • Incorrect. This is the probability that the student takes less than 51 minutes (\(P(X \le 51)\)). Remember to use the complement rule to find the area of the upper tail for "more than."
    • \(0.0139\)
    • Correct! First, standardize the target value to find the Z-score:
      \begin{equation*} Z = \frac{51 - 40}{5} = \frac{11}{5} = 2.2 \end{equation*}
      Using a standard normal table or technology, the area to the left of \(Z = 2.2\) is \(0.9861\text{.}\) Finding the upper tail gives:
      \begin{equation*} P(X \gt 51) = P(Z \gt 2.2) = 1 - 0.9861 = 0.0139 \end{equation*}
    • \(0.0179\)
    • Incorrect. It looks like you might have used the variance (\(\sigma^2 = 25\)) in the denominator instead of the standard deviation (\(\sigma = 5\)) when calculating your Z-score.
    • \(0.1333\)
    • Incorrect. This was the answer from the uniform distribution version of this problem. Under a normal curve, the probability density drops significantly as you move out into the tails.
    • \(0.0228\)
    • Incorrect. This is the probability of a value falling more than 2 standard deviations away (\(Z \gt 2.0\)). Recheck your subtraction arithmetic for the Z-score numerator.
    🔗
    (b)
    Find the probability that a randomly selected student completes the exam in a time frame between 31 and 35 minutes (\(P(31 \le X \le 35)\)).
    🔗
    • \(0.1227\)
    • Correct! First, convert both boundary thresholds into their respective standard normal Z-scores:
      \begin{equation*} Z_1 = \frac{31 - 40}{5} = -1.8 \quad \text{and} \quad Z_2 = \frac{35 - 40}{5} = -1.0 \end{equation*}
      Find the cumulative area to the left for both scores from your standard normal distribution curve tables:
      \begin{equation*} P(Z \le -1.0) = 0.1587 \quad \text{and} \quad P(Z \le -1.8) = 0.0360 \end{equation*}
      Subtracting the lower boundary area from the upper boundary area yields the interval probability:
      \begin{equation*} P(-1.8 \le Z \le -1.0) = 0.1587 - 0.0360 = 0.1227 \end{equation*}
    • \(0.1587\)
    • Incorrect. This is only the cumulative area to the left of 35 minutes (\(P(X \le 35)\)). You still need to subtract the lower tail area corresponding to times under 31 minutes.
    • \(0.1947\)
    • Incorrect. It looks like you added the two boundary probabilities together instead of subtracting them to find the area contained between them.
    • \(0.1333\)
    • Incorrect. Remember that unlike the uniform distribution, intervals of equal width do not have equal probabilities in a normal distribution because the curve is non-linear and changes height.
    • \(0.0360\)
    • Incorrect. This is the lower tail probability of a student completing the exam in fewer than 31 minutes (\(P(X \lt 31)\)).

    3. Continuous Distributions: Hybrid Fuel Efficiency.

    A new gas- and electric-powered hybrid car has recently hit the consumer automotive market. The distance traveled on one gallon of fuel is modeled by a normal distribution with a mean of 65 miles and a standard deviation of 8 miles. If one car is tested at random, compute the following probabilities.
    🔗
    (a)
    Find the probability that the car travels more than 71 miles per gallon.
    🔗
    • \(0.7734\)
    • Incorrect. This is the probability that the car travels less than 71 miles per gallon (\(P(X \le 71)\)). Remember to find the upper tail area for "more than."
    • \(0.2266\)
    • Correct! First, find the standard normal Z-score:
      \begin{equation*} Z = \frac{71 - 65}{8} = \frac{6}{8} = 0.75 \end{equation*}
      The cumulative area to the left of \(Z = 0.75\) is \(0.7734\text{.}\) Subtracting from 1 to find the upper tail gives:
      \begin{equation*} P(X \gt 71) = P(Z \gt 0.75) = 1 - 0.7734 = 0.2266 \end{equation*}
    • \(0.2734\)
    • Incorrect. Double-check your numerator subtraction and division when standardizing your raw score into a Z-score.
    • \(0.1587\)
    • Incorrect. This matches a Z-score of exactly \(1.0\text{.}\) Make sure to divide by the standard deviation parameter \(\sigma = 8\text{.}\)
    • \(0.4701\)
    • Incorrect. This looks like the raw area between the mean and the target score instead of the remaining tail probability.
    🔗
    (b)
    Find the probability that the car travels less than 58 miles per gallon.
    🔗
    • \(0.1908\)
    • Correct! Convert the threshold parameter into a standard normal Z-score:
      \begin{equation*} Z = \frac{58 - 65}{8} = \frac{-7}{8} = -0.875 \approx -0.88 \end{equation*}
      Looking up the lower tail area directly to the left of \(Z = -0.88\) yields:
      \begin{equation*} P(X \lt 58) = P(Z \lt -0.88) \approx 0.1894 \quad \text{(or } 0.1908 \text{ unrounded)} \end{equation*}
    • \(0.8092\)
    • Incorrect. This is the probability that the vehicle gets more than 58 miles per gallon (\(P(X \ge 58)\)).
    • \(0.2119\)
    • Incorrect. It looks like you rounded your Z-score aggressively to \(-0.80\) or \(-0.85\) instead of evaluating \(-0.88\text{.}\)
    • \(0.3106\)
    • Incorrect. This matches a Z-score calculation slip where the denominator was misidentified. Double-check your parameter targets.
    • \(0.0548\)
    • Incorrect. This represents a value significantly further out in the distribution tail than 58 miles per gallon.

    (c)
    Find the probability that the car travels between 57 and 73 miles per gallon.
    🔗
    • \(0.3413\)
    • Incorrect. This is only the probability area between the mean and one standard deviation above the mean.
    • \(0.8413\)
    • Incorrect. This is only the cumulative area to the left of the upper bound (\(P(X \le 73)\)). Remember to subtract the lower tail area below 57 mpg.
    • \(0.6826\)
    • Correct! Convert both boundary limits into standard normal Z-scores:
      \begin{equation*} Z_1 = \frac{57 - 65}{8} = \frac{-8}{8} = -1.0 \quad \text{and} \quad Z_2 = \frac{73 - 65}{8} = \frac{8}{8} = 1.0 \end{equation*}
      Notice that these bounds map precisely to exactly \(\pm 1\) standard deviation away from the distribution mean. Applying the Empirical Rule (the 68-95-99.7 rule), any symmetric normal interval within one standard deviation of the center encompasses approximately 68% of total data entries:
      \begin{equation*} P(-1.0 \le Z \le 1.0) = 0.8413 - 0.1587 = 0.6826 \end{equation*}
    • \(0.9544\)
    • Incorrect. This is the empirical probability contained within two standard deviations of the mean (\(\pm 2\sigma\)).
    • \(0.4972\)
    • Incorrect. This matches an asymmetric interval evaluation error. Review your standard lookup tables for \(Z = 1.0\) and \(Z = -1.0\text{.}\)

    (d)
    A group of eco-conscious drivers are competing in a "hypermiling" distance challenge using this hybrid model to see who can get the most out of a single gallon of gas under various driving conditions. To earn an elite efficiency certificate, a driver’s trip must rank in the top 5% of all recorded fuel economy outcomes for this vehicle. Find the minimum miles per gallon a participant must achieve to qualify for the certificate.
    🔗
    • \(51.84 \text{ mpg}\)
    • Incorrect. It looks like you subtracted the value from the mean (\(65 - 1.645 \cdot 8\)). This gives the threshold for the bottom 5% of fuel efficiency rather than the top 5%.
    • \(75.20 \text{ mpg}\)
    • Incorrect. This calculation uses a Z-score of \(Z = 1.28\text{,}\) which corresponds to the top 10% (the 90th percentile) instead of the top 5% (the 95th percentile).
    • \(78.16 \text{ mpg}\)
    • Correct! Being in the top 5% means that 95% of all cars have a lower fuel efficiency (the 95th percentile). Looking up a cumulative area of \(0.9500\) in a standard normal distribution table gives a critical Z-score of approximately \(Z = 1.645\text{.}\)
      Now, use the inverse transformation formula to solve for the raw value \(x\text{:}\)
      \begin{equation*} x = \mu + Z \cdot \sigma = 65 + 1.645 \cdot 8 = 65 + 13.16 = 78.16 \text{ mpg} \end{equation*}
    • \(81.44 \text{ mpg}\)
    • Incorrect. This calculation uses a Z-score of \(Z = 2.056\text{,}\) which maps to the top 2% of the data distribution instead of the targeted top 5% tier.
    • \(65.05 \text{ mpg}\)
    • Incorrect. This value is barely above the distribution mean of 65. Remember that achieving a top 5% metric requires a value significantly further out in the positive direction of the distribution curve.
