    1. Technical Interview.

      A computer science student prepares for a technical interview by studying fourteen provided coding challenges. The student was able to complete eight of the challenges. During the interview, six of the coding challenges are selected to test the student’s knowledge.
      🔗
      Match each block on the left with its correct contextual meaning on the right. Ultimately, you are trying to find the probability that the student can complete four of the six coding challenges selected during their interview.
      🔗
    • \(\binom{14}{6} = 3,003\)
    • The total number of ways that six questions can be selected for the student’s interview.
    • \(\binom{8}{4} = 70\)
    • The number of ways that 4 of the coding challenges that the student completed can be selected for the student’s interview.
    • \(\binom{6}{2} = 15\)
    • The number of ways that 2 of the coding challenges that the student had not completed can be selected for the student’s interview.
    • \(70 \times 15 = 1,050\)
    • The total number of ways that six problems can be selected for the interview where the student can complete four of them.
    • \(\frac{1050}{3003} = \frac{50}{143}\)
    • The probability that the student can complete four of the six coding challenges selected during their interview.
    Hint.
    Think of the 14 problems as being split into two sets - those the student could complete and those that they had not mastered yet.

    2. Campus Housing Room Assignments.

    A group of \(12\) college students are selected to live on a specialized floor in a residence hall. The floor has exactly \(6\) rooms available that must be filled: one triple room (\(3\) students), three double rooms (\(2\) students each), and three single rooms (\(1\) student each).
    🔗
    If the housing department assigns the \(12\) students completely at random to fill these rooms, how many distinct ways can the students be assigned to rooms?
    🔗
    • \(\frac{12!}{3!\cdot 2!\cdot 2!\cdot 2!\cdot 1!\cdot 1!\cdot 1!} \) ways
    • Correct! Because the rooms themselves are distinct entities, we use the multinomial coefficient to partition the 12 individuals into groups of sizes 3, 2, 2, 2, 1, 1, and 1.
    • \(\binom{12}{3\ 2\ 2\ 2\ 1\ 1\ 1} \)
    • Correct! Because the rooms themselves are distinct entities, we use the multinomial coefficient to partition the 12 individuals into groups of sizes 3, 2, 2, 2, 1, 1, and 1.
    • \(\binom{12}{3}\times \binom{9}{2}\times \binom{7}{2} \times \binom{5}{2} \times \binom{3}{1} \times \binom{2}{1} \times 1\)
    • Correct! Because the rooms themselves are distinct entities, but order does not matter within rooms, we can use combinations to partition the 12 individuals into groups of sizes 3, 2, 2, 2, 1, 1, and 1. This calculation is equivalent to using the multinomial coefficient.
    • \(12! \)
    • Incorrect. This answer over counts, because rearranging students within the same room does not create a new room assignment configuration.
    • \(\binom{12}{3} \times \binom{9}{6} \times \binom{3}{3} \)
    • Incorrect. This calculation treats the three double rooms as a single giant group and the three single rooms as a single giant group, rather than distributing students into separate rooms.
    • \(\binom{12}{3} \times \binom{9}{2} \times \binom{7}{1} \)
    • Incorrect. This calculation only assigns students to the triple room, one double room, and one single room. The other rooms are not accounted for.
    • \(\frac{12!}{3!\cdot 3!\cdot 3!} \)
    • Incorrect. This answer attempts to use the multinomial coefficient, but the numbers in the denominator have been chosen incorrectly.
    Hint.
    Think about partitioning a single pool of \(12\) distinct people into multiple specific groups. The group sizes are defined by the room capacities.

    3. Poker Hands.

    A standard deck of 52 cards features 13 distinct numerical ranks (Ace, 2, 3,...,10, Jack, Queen, King) in each of 4 distinct suits (Hearts, Diamonds, Clubs, and Spades).
    🔗
    In a standard 5-card poker match, a player is dealt a hand of 5 cards completely at random from the deck of 52.
    🔗
    (a)
    🔗
    (b)
    What would be your strategy for computing the probability of not being dealt any of the poker hands? This is also referred to as a “High Card” hand.
    🔗
    Select the equation that correctly represents the strategic approach for computing the number of possible High Card hands?
    🔗
    • \(\binom{13}{5} \cdot 4^5\)
    • Incorrect. This expression counts the number of ways to get a hand containing cards of 5 completely different ranks, each assigned a random suit. This method would count "Straights" (5 consecutive ranks) and "Flushes" (all 5 cards sharing the same suit), which cannot be part of a High Card hand.
    • \(\left[ \binom{13}{5} - 10 \right] \cdot (4^5 - 4)\)
    • Correct! One way to build a High Card hand is to pick 5 distinct ranks out of 13, but you must subtract off the 10 possible rank sequences that form a Straight. Then, you assign suits to those cards, but you must subtract the 4 suit configurations that would result in a flush (all cards share the same suit).
    • \(\binom{13}{5} \cdot4^5 - 40\)
    • Incorrect. This expression counts the number of ways to get a hand containing cards of 5 completely different ranks, each assigned a random suit. Then this method tries to handle the overlap with Straights and Flushes by subtracting a constant of 40. However, this only removes the Straight Flushes, rather than all Straights and all Flushes.
    • \(\binom{13}{1}\binom{4}{1} \cdot \binom{12}{1}\binom{4}{1} \cdot \binom{11}{1}\binom{4}{1} \cdot \binom{10}{1}\binom{4}{1} \cdot \binom{9}{1}\binom{4}{1}\)
    • Incorrect. This expression tracks the dealt order of the cards. Because poker hands are unordered, this counts identical hands multiple times and fails to filter out Straights and Flushes.
    Hint.
    Think about what is or is not being selected with each step of the answers. Could you get one of the Poker hands with the selection?

    4. Poker Hands with Jokers.

    Refer to the previous problem: Poker Hands.
    🔗
    Suppose that two Jokers are added to the deck, bringing the total deck size to 54 cards. These are wild cards and can count as any rank or suit.
    🔗
    Answer the questions below to find the probability of being dealt a "Three of a Kind" in this new scenario, where your hand could include 0, 1, or 2 Jokers.
    🔗
    (a)
    Calculate the number of 5-card hands that contain exactly 1 Joker and form a Three of a Kind.
    🔗
    Note: If a Joker can optimize a hand into a higher category (like a Full House or Four of a Kind), it must.
    🔗
    • \(\binom{13}{1}\binom{4}{3}\binom{12}{2}4^2\binom{2}{1} = 109,824\) hands
    • Incorrect. This expression gives us a triple in the four non-Joker cards. If you add a wild Joker, you end up with a "Four of a Kind."
    • \(\binom{13}{1}\binom{4}{2}\binom{12}{2}4^2\binom{2}{1} = 164,736\) hands
    • Correct! To form a Three of a Kind with 1 Joker, the other 4 cards must consist of a natural pair and two separate singletons of unique ranks (e.g., 10-10-4-7 + Joker). The calculation evaluates as follows:
      • Choose 1 rank out of 13 for the pair: \(\binom{13}{1} \)
      • Choose 2 of 4 cards available of that rank (to complete the selection of the pair): \(\binom{4}{2} \)
      • Choose 2 unique ranks out of the remaining 12 for reamining non-Joker cards: \(\binom{12}{2} \)
      • Choose 1 suit out of 4 for each singleton: \(4^2 \)
      • Choose 1 Joker out of the 2 available in the deck: \(\binom{2}{1} = 2\)
    • \(\binom{13}{1}\binom{4}{2}\binom{12}{1}\binom{4}{2}\binom{2}{1} = 5,616\) hands
    • Incorrect. This expression counts the number of hands consisting of two pairs and 1 Joker (e.g., 8-8-3-3 + Joker). The Joker turns this hand into a "Full House" instead of a Three of a Kind.
    • \(\binom{13}{4}4^4\binom{2}{1} = 366,080\) hands
    • Incorrect. This expression counts the number of hands where the 4 non-Joker cards all have a unique rank (e.g., A-K-Q-5 + Joker). The Joker turns this hand into a "One Pair" rather than a Three of a Kind.
    • \(\binom{52}{4}\binom{2}{1} = 541,450\) hands
    • Incorrect. This broadly counts every 5-card hand that contains exactly 1 Joker. It ignores what poker hand combination those cards actually form.
    Hint.
    To avoid completing a better hand, the 4 non-Joker cards must consist of one natural pair and two cards that each possess different, non-matching ranks.

    7. Dice Frequencies.

    We have two dice, a fair 6-sided die and a fair 10-sided die. The six sided die is rolled 14 times in a row. The 10-sided die is rolled 8 times in a row. Each individual roll is completely independent of the others.
    🔗
    (a)
    Consider the 6-sided die only. Which of the following expressions correctly counts the number of 14-roll sequences containing exactly 4 ones and 4 twos?
    🔗
    • \(\binom{14}{4}\binom{10}{4}4^6\)
    • Correct! First, choose 4 positions out of the 14 available slots for the ones: \(\binom{14}{4}\text{.}\) Next, choose 4 positions out of the remaining 10 vacant slots for the twos: \(\binom{10}{4}\text{.}\) Then fill the remaining 6 slots with any of the 4 other die numbers (3, 4, 5, or 6), which gives \(4^6\) possibilities. Multiplying these together gives the total number of sequences.
    • \(\binom{14}{4}\binom{14}{4}4^6\)
    • Incorrect. After placing the 4 ones (or twos), there are only 10 available slots left in the sequence, not 14.
    • \(\binom{14}{4}\binom{10}{4}6^6\)
    • Incorrect. The remaining 6 positions can only be filled by numbers other than 1 and 2. This leaves exactly 4 options (3, 4, 5, and 6) for each remaining slot.
    • \(\binom{14}{4} + \binom{10}{4} + 4^6\)
    • Incorrect. By the Multiplication Principle, the components of the answer must be multiplied together rather than added.
    • \(6^{14}\)
    • Incorrect. This counts the size of the sample space.
