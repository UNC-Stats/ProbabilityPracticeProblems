    1. Wellness Program Prediabetes Screening.

    As part of a corporate wellness program, employees at a large firm are being screened for indicators of prediabetes using an HbA1c blood test. Human Resources has been requested to identify and refer exactly three employees who show positive indicators of prediabetes to an onsite health coach for preventative tracking. According to national health statistics, approximately 40% of the adult employee population possesses positive indicators for prediabetes.
    🔗
    (a)
      Match each question with its correct numeric answer.
      🔗
    • What is the probability that exactly 6 employees must be tested in order to identify the 3 required positive individuals?
    • \(0.138\)
    • What is the probability that at most 6 employees must be tested in order to identify the 3 required positive individuals?
    • \(0.455\)
    • What is the probability that the team tests exactly 2 negative employees before successfully locating the first positive individual.
    • \(0.144\)
    🔗
    (b)
    🔗
    (c)
    Suppose that instead of testing sequentially, the team decides to just randomly select a group of ten employees from the company database and test them all simultaneously. What is the probability that they find at least three positive results?
    🔗
    • \(0.121\)
    • Incorrect. This is only the probability of finding exactly two positive results, \(P(X = 2)\text{.}\) The question asks for at least three positives.
    • \(0.167\)
    • Incorrect. This is the cumulative probability of finding two or fewer positives, \(P(X \le 2)\text{.}\) You need to subtract this value from 1 to find the complement.
    • \(0.833\)
    • Correct! Because the sample size is fixed at \(n = 10\text{,}\) this follows a Binomial distribution. To find \(P(X \ge 3)\text{,}\) use the complement rule:
      \begin{equation*} P(X \ge 3) = 1 - P(X \le 2) = 1 - [P(X=0) + P(X=1) + P(X=2)] \end{equation*}
      Calculating individual baseline probabilities:
      \begin{align*} P(X=0) \amp = \binom{10}{0}(0.40)^0(0.60)^{10} \approx 0.006\\ P(X=1) \amp = \binom{10}{1}(0.40)^1(0.60)^9 \approx 0.040\\ P(X=2) \amp = \binom{10}{2}(0.40)^2(0.60)^8 \approx 0.121 \end{align*}
      Subtract the sum of these probabilities from 1:
      \begin{equation*} P(X \ge 3) = 1 - (0.006 + 0.040 + 0.121) = 1 - 0.167 = 0.833 \end{equation*}
    • \(0.215\)
    • Incorrect. This is the probability of finding exactly three positive results, \(P(X = 3)\text{.}\) Remember that "at least three" means you must include the probabilities for 3, 4, 5, ..., up to 10 successes.
    • \(0.785\)
    • Incorrect. This is the complement of finding exactly three positive results, \(1-P(X = 3)\text{.}\) Remember that "at least three" means you must include the probabilities for 3, 4, 5, ..., up to 10 successes. Your computation removes 3 and adds 0,1,2.

    • A Bernoulli random variable is strictly limited to an outcome space containing exactly two binary values (usually coded as \(0\) and \(1\)), whereas \(X\) has seven distinct outcomes.
    • Correct! A Bernoulli distribution is defined exclusively over a binary outcome trial (success or failure). Because \(X\) can track seven separate target locations, it does not fit a single Bernoulli structure.
    • Each transaction trial behaves independently and can be run over and over again without replacement limits.

    • The sum of all probabilities in the uniform support pool adds up to 1.

    • No, because a Bernoulli variable requires drawing items without replacement, which forces probabilities to shift dynamically.
    • Incorrect. You are confusing a Bernoulli trial with a hypergeometric draw. Bernoulli trials must have independent setups with a constant probability of success across binary options.
    • Yes, because any multi-categorical model can be classified as a Bernoulli variable as long as the sample space is finite.
    • Incorrect. A finite sample space is required for any discrete variable, but a Bernoulli distribution specifically restricts the number of categories to exactly two.
    • No, because the expected value of a Bernoulli variable must always be greater than 1, which is impossible given a uniform sample size of seven.
    • Incorrect. The expected value of a Bernoulli random variable is simply its success probability \(p\text{,}\) which must always fall strictly between 0 and 1.

    • Binomial with parameters \(n = 5\) and \(p = \frac{1}{7}\text{.}\)
    • Correct! We are counting the total number of successes (routing to Alpha) across a fixed sequence of \(n = 5\) independent trials, where each trial has a constant binary success probability of \(p = \frac{1}{7}\text{.}\)
    • Geometric distribution with parameter \(p = \frac{1}{7}\text{.}\)
    • Incorrect. A geometric model tracks the number of attempts needed to reach the *very first* success. Here, we are observing a fixed window of exactly 5 trials and counting the total accumulation of successes.
    • Hypergeometric distribution with parameters \(N = 7\text{,}\) \(n = 5\text{,}\) and \(k = 1\text{.}\)
    • Incorrect. Hypergeometric distributions apply when sampling occurs *without replacement*, causing the odds to shift. Because the routing algorithm treats each choice independently, the parameters do not alter between runs.
    • Negative Binomial distribution with parameters \(r = 5\) and \(p = \frac{1}{7}\text{.}\)
    • Incorrect. A negative binomial distribution counts the number of failures or total trials encountered before accumulating a fixed number of target successes r, which is not the case here.
    • Discrete Uniform distribution with parameter k = 5.
    • Incorrect. While the underlying single selection X is uniform, the aggregated count of occurrences Y across multiple independent trials is not equally likely across its values (e.g., getting zero successes is far more likely than getting three).

    3.

    In a remote wildlife sanctuary, conservation biologists are auditing a small batch of animal tracking collars before deployment. An inventory box contains a total of \(10\) tracking collars: \(6\) are advanced GPS-enabled collars and \(4\) are traditional VHF radio collars. A researcher randomly selects \(4\) collars from the box to take into the field. Let \(Y\) be the number of GPS-enabled collars selected.
    🔗
    (a)
    Part A: What type of random variable is \(Y\text{?}\) Explain your reasoning.
    🔗
    • Hypergeometric
    • Correct! The selection is done without replacement from a finite population (\(N=10\)) containing a specific number of successes (\(K=6\) GPS collars). The trials are dependent.
    • Binomial
    • Incorrect. Because the selection is made without replacement from a very small finite population, the probability of choosing a GPS collar changes with each pick.
    • Geometric
    • Incorrect. A geometric variable counts the number of trials until the first success occurs, whereas we have a fixed sample size of \(n=4\text{.}\)
    🔗
    (b)
    Part B: Find the probability that three or more GPS-enabled collars are selected.
    🔗
    • \(0.3810\)
    • Incorrect. This is only the probability of getting exactly 3 GPS collars: \(P(Y = 3)\text{.}\)
    • \(0.4524\)
    • Correct! We sum the individual probabilities for \(Y=3\) and \(Y=4\text{:}\)
      \begin{equation*} P(Y \ge 3) = \frac{\binom{6}{3}\binom{4}{1}}{\binom{10}{4}} + \frac{\binom{6}{4}\binom{4}{0}}{\binom{10}{4}} = \frac{80}{210} + \frac{15}{210} = \frac{95}{210} \approx 0.4524 \end{equation*}
    • \(0.0714\)
    • Incorrect. This is only the probability of getting exactly 4 GPS collars: \(P(Y = 4)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    (c)
    Part C: What are the mean (\(\mu\)) and standard deviation (\(\sigma\)) for \(Y\text{?}\)
    🔗
    • \(\mu = 2.4\text{,}\) \(\sigma = 0.8000\)
    • Correct! The mean is calculated as: \(\mu = n \left(\frac{K}{N}\right) = 4 \left(\frac{6}{10}\right) = 2.4\text{.}\) The variance is: \(\sigma^2 = n \left(\frac{K}{N}\right)\left(\frac{N-K}{N}\right)\left(\frac{N-n}{N-1}\right) = 4(0.6)(0.4)\left(\frac{6}{9}\right) = 0.64\text{.}\) The standard deviation is: \(\sigma = \sqrt{0.64} = 0.8000\text{.}\)
    • \(\mu = 2.4\text{,}\) \(\sigma = 0.6400\)
    • Incorrect. You found the variance (\(\sigma^2\)) instead of taking its square root for the standard deviation.
    • \(\mu = 2.0\text{,}\) \(\sigma = 1.0000\)
    • Incorrect. Please recheck the formulas using parameters \(N=10\text{,}\) \(K=6\text{,}\) and \(n=4\text{.}\)

    4. Epidemiology Study: E. Coli Dynamics.

    Increased medical research has focused on tracking severe illnesses caused by the organism Escherichia coli (O157:H7), a bacterium that can trigger intestinal hemorrhages and a critical breakdown of red blood cells. Historical epidemiology data shows that sporadic outbreaks of E.coli have occurred in Colorado at a rate of approximately 2.4 per 100,000 per year.
    🔗
    If this rate has not changed and if 100,000 cases from Colorado are reviewed for this year, what is the probability that 5 cases of E.coli will be observed? Complete the following questions to find the answer:
    🔗
    (a)
    Define a random variable \(X\) for this scenario and specify what type of random variable it is.
    🔗
    • \(X\) represents the number of reviews required until the first E. coli case is found; it is a Geometric random variable.
    • Incorrect. A geometric model would look at the number of sequential patients screened until a positive case is encountered, rather than counting total instances in a fixed population sample.
    • \(X\) represents the proportion of infected individuals out of the 100,000 screened; it is a Uniform random variable.
    • Incorrect. Uniform random variables model experiments where every discrete outcome has an identical probability weight, or areas where probabilities are flat across an interval.
    • \(X\) represents the exact count of E. coli cases observed in the sample of 100,000 people over the year; it is a Poisson random variable.
    • Correct! Because we are tracking the number of occurrences of a rare event within a specified, fixed continuum of space and time (100,000 people over one year) with a known constant average rate, it is a Poisson process.
    • \(X\) represents whether a single randomly chosen case has an E. coli infection or not; it is a Binomial random variable with \(n=1\text{.}\)
    • Incorrect. A single binary trial would be a Bernoulli trial, but we are aggregating counts across a vast population group.
    • \(X\) represents the number of positive E. coli cases out of exactly 5 selected files; it is a Hypergeometric random variable.
    • Incorrect. While we are looking for 5 cases in the final step, \(Z\) itself must be capable of tracking any count of outbreaks across the entire 100,000-person continuum.
    🔗
    (b)
    Which of the following functions correctly specifies the probability mass function (PMF) for your random variable \(X\text{?}\)
    🔗
    • \(p(x) = \frac{e^{-2.4} \cdot (2.4)^x}{x!}\)
    • Correct! The baseline rate is 2.4 per 100,000, and our sample group matches that unit size exactly (100,000 people). Therefore, the expected value parameter is \(\lambda = 2.4\text{.}\)
    • \(p(x) = \binom{100000}{x}(0.000024)^x(0.999976)^{100000-x}\)
    • Incorrect. While a Binomial distribution can model vast population sets, evaluating a binomial expansion with an \(n\) of 100,000 is mathematically inefficient and outside the scope of utilizing standard Poisson interval parameters directly.
    • \(p(x) = \frac{e^{-100000} \cdot (100000)^x}{x!}\)
    • Incorrect. You used the size of the population sample (\(100,000\)) as the expected arrival rate \(\lambda\text{,}\) instead of the actual average number of cases (\(2.4\)).
    • \(p(X) = (0.000024)(0.999976)^{x-1}\)
    • Incorrect. This matches a geometric waiting distribution style rather than a Poisson count distribution function.
    • \(p(x) = \frac{e^{-5} \cdot (5)^x}{x!}\)
    • Incorrect. You used the target value of interest (\(x=5\) cases) as the expected mean parameter \(\lambda\text{.}\) The average rate remains \(2.4\text{.}\)

    5. Discrete Distributions: Clinical Trial Therapeutic Efficacy.

    Historical epidemiological data demonstrates that \(55\%\) of all patients afflicted by a specific chronic inflammatory illness can successfully achieve temporary remission and recover naturally without any clinical intervention.
    🔗
    A biomedical research group develops an innovative targeted therapeutic treatment. They randomly select \(10\) diagnosed patients and administer the new treatment; \(9\) of these patients achieve full clinical remission shortly thereafter. To analyze whether the new therapy provides a statistically significant benefit, assume the baseline hypothesis that the treatment is completely ineffective and worthless.
    🔗
    (a)
    Let the random variable \(Y\) represent the total number of patients who successfully achieve remission out of the 10 selected. Under the assumption that the new treatment is completely ineffective, what type of random variable is \(Y\text{?}\)
    🔗
    • Binomial distribution, because there is a fixed number of independent patient observations (\(n = 10\)), each trial results in a binary outcome (recovers or fails to recover), and the baseline recovery probability remains constant at \(p = 0.55\text{.}\)
    • Correct! Because we assume the treatment is worthless, the probability of recovery on each trial stays locked at the natural background rate of \(0.55\text{.}\) Since the patient responses behave independently and the total sample size is fixed at \(10\text{,}\) this satisfies all criteria for a binomial model.
    • Geometric distribution, because we are measuring a sequence of binary recovery outcomes across a group of clinical subjects.
    • Incorrect. A geometric model counts the number of separate trials required to reach the very first success. Here, the number of total trials is pre-fixed at 10, and we are counting the total accumulated successes.
    • Hypergeometric distribution, because we are selecting a subset of clinical subjects out of a specific regional medical facility pool.
    • Incorrect. Hypergeometric distributions apply when sampling is conducted without replacement from a small, finite total population, causing probabilities to shift on each draw. The problem specifies a random selection from an overall large background patient environment.
    • Poisson distribution, because we are counting patient recoveries that occur over a fixed window of clinical observation time.
    • Incorrect. While time is mentioned conceptually, the mathematical support is explicitly a count of successes across a finite, bounded pool of 10 individual patient subjects.
    🔗
    (b)
    Hint.

Observing 9 patient recoveries is higher than the natural expected average of 5.5, and the probability of getting 9 or more recoveries completely by chance is low (\(P \approx 0.0233\)), indicating the therapy is highly likely to be genuinely effective.
