    5. Ticket Raffle.

    A high-demand campus music festival has a limited ticket lottery. There are \(150\) students who enter the raffle, and the event organizers choose \(15\) winners at random.
    🔗
    What is the probability that either you or your friend Brenda (or both of you) are among the chosen winners?
    🔗
    • \(0.191\)
    • Correct! The easiest way to calculate this is by using the complement rule.
      \begin{equation*} P(\text{neither wins})=\dfrac{\binom{148}{15}}{\binom{150}{15}}\text{,} \end{equation*}
      since the number of ways to pick 15 winners entirely from the other 148 students is \(\binom{148}{15}\text{.}\) Applying the complement rule, we have
      \begin{equation*} P(\text{you or Brenda wins})=1-P(\text{neither wins}) = 1 - 0.809 = 0.191\text{.} \end{equation*}
    • \(0.809\)
    • Incorrect. This is the probability that neither you nor Brenda win \(\left(\frac{\binom{148}{15}}{\binom{150}{15}} = 0.809\right)\text{.}\) You must subtract this value from 1 to find the probability that at least one of you is chosen.
    • \(0.200\)
    • Incorrect. This uses the ratio of winners to total entrants for the probability that each of you win, ignoring the other person (\(\frac{15}{150} + \frac{15}{150} = 0.20\)). This approach over-counts the scenario where both of you win.
    • \(0.009\)
    • Incorrect. This is the probability that both of you win!
    • \(0.798\)
    • Incorrect. This uses the ratio of winners to total entrants that are not you and Brenda to try to compute the probability of the complement (\(2 \times \frac{15}{148}\)). This approach is not correct.
    Hint.
    Think about the complement of the event. It is much easier to compute the probability that neither you nor Brenda are selected as winners.

    8. Roster Analytics in a Superhero Franchise.

    A pop culture media analyst is studying the character roster of a massive superhero comic book publishing franchise. A character is pulled completely at random from the active roster database and evaluated based on two specific traits: they are either a Marvel character (\(M\)) or a DC character (\(D\)), and they are either a Hero (\(H\)) or a Villain (\(V\)).
    🔗
    The character sample space therefore contains four distinct, mutually exclusive outcomes:
    \begin{equation*} S = \{(M,H), (M,V), (D,H), (D,V)\} \end{equation*}
    Let \(A\) denote the event that a Marvel character is selected, and let \(B\) denote the event that a Hero is selected. Franchise metrics show that \(P(A) = 0.70\) and \(P(B) = 0.55\text{.}\) In other words, 70% of the roster are Marvel properties and 55% of the roster are classified as Heroes.
    🔗
    (a)
    Given the above information, what is the largest possible value for the probability of the the intersection, \(P(A \cap B)\text{?}\) (i.e., What is the maximum possible proportion of Marvel Heroes?)
    🔗
    • \(1.00\)
    • Incorrect. The intersection cannot exceed the probability of either individual event since \((A \cap B) \subseteq A\) and \((A \cap B) \subseteq B\text{.}\)
    • \(0.70\)
    • Incorrect. If the intersection were 0.70, it would mean there are more Marvel Heroes than there are total Heroes in the entire database (\(P(B) = 0.55\)), which is mathematically impossible.
    • \(0.55\)
    • Correct! The maximum possible value for an intersection is capped by the smaller of the two individual event probabilities. This maximum is only reached if \(B \subseteq A\text{,}\) meaning every single Hero in the database happens to belong to the Marvel universe.
    • \(0.25\)
    • Incorrect. This is the smallest possible value for the probability of the intersection.
    • \(0.39\)
    • Incorrect. This is the intersection value if the two events were completely independent (\(0.70 \cdot 0.55 = 0.39\)). However, the question asks for the maximum possible value.
    🔗
    (b)
    Hint.
    Assuming the maximum value, then every hero in the database belongs to the Marvel universe.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    (c)
    Given the provided probabilities for \(A\) and \(B\text{,}\) what is the smallest possible value for the intersection probability, \(P(A \cap B)\text{?}\) (i.e., What is the minimum possible proportion of Marvel Heroes?)
    🔗
    • \(0\)
    • Incorrect. If the intersection were 0, the union probability would be \(P(A \cup B) = 0.70 + 0.55 = 1.25\text{.}\) This violates Probability Axiom 1 that states a probability cannot exceed 1.
    • \(0.25\)
    • Correct! By the Inclusion-Exclusion Principle, we know \(P(A \cup B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A \cap B) = 1.25 - P(A \cap B)\text{.}\) Because \(P(A \cup B)\leq 1 \text{,}\) the intersection probability must be at least \(0.25\text{.}\)
    • \(0.15\)
    • Incorrect. This is too small! Try using the Inclusion-Exclusion Principle.
    • \(0.55\)
    • Incorrect. This is the largest possible value for the probability of the intersection.
    • \(0.30\)
    • Incorrect. Try using the Inclusion-Exclusion Principle.
