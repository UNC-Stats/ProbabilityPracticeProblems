    1. Properties of Continuous Density Functions.

    Consider a continuous random variable \(Y\) defined by the probability density function (PDF):
    \begin{equation*} f_Y(y) = ky^2, \quad \text{for } 0 \le y \le 2\text{,} \end{equation*}
    where \(k\) is a constant.
    🔗
    (a)
    What must the value of the constant \(k\) equal for \(f_Y(y)\) to be a mathematically valid probability density function?
    🔗
    • \(k = 1/2\)
    • Incorrect. Remember that the total area under the curve from \(y=0\) to \(y=2\) must integrate to exactly 1.
    • \(k = 1/8\)
    • Incorrect. It looks like you evaluated the anti-derivative boundaries but forgot to incorporate the denominator from the power rule integration.
    • \(k = 3/8\)
    • Correct! Using the total area property:
      \begin{equation*} \int_{0}^{2} ky^2 \, dy = \left[ \frac{k y^3}{3} \right]_{0}^{2} = \frac{8k}{3} - 0 = 1 \implies k = \frac{3}{8} \end{equation*}
    • \(k = 3/4\)
    • Incorrect. Double-check your calculation when evaluating \(y^3\) at the upper bound of 2.
    🔗
    (b)
    Compute the expected value \(E(Y)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    • \(E(Y) = 1.00\)
    • Incorrect. Remember that for a continuous variable, \(E(Y) = \int y \cdot f_Y(y) \, dy\text{,}\) which shifts the integrand power to \(y^3\text{.}\)
    • \(E(Y) = 1.50\)
    • Correct! Setting up the expectation integral:
      \begin{equation*} E(Y) = \int_{0}^{2} y \cdot \left(\frac{3}{8}y^2\right) \, dy = \frac{3}{8} \int_{0}^{2} y^3 \, dy = \frac{3}{8} \left[ \frac{y^4}{4} \right]_{0}^{2} = \frac{3}{8} \left(\frac{16}{4}\right) = 1.5 \end{equation*}
    • \(E(Y) = 1.33\)
    • Incorrect. This looks like the result of calculating the anti-derivative without scaling by the constant factor \(k = 3/8\text{.}\)
    • \(E(Y) = 1.75\)
    • Incorrect. Double-check your evaluation steps when raising the upper bound to the 4th power.
    🔗
    (c)
    Compute the variance \(V(Y)\) for this random variable.
    🔗
    • \(V(Y) = 0.15\)
    • Correct! First, compute the intermediate term \(E(Y^2)\text{:}\)
      \begin{equation*} E(Y^2) = \int_{0}^{2} y^2 \cdot \left(\frac{3}{8}y^2\right) \, dy = \frac{3}{8} \left[ \frac{y^5}{5} \right]_{0}^{2} = \frac{3}{8} \left(\frac{32}{5}\right) = \frac{12}{5} = 2.4 \end{equation*}
      Now, use the variance identity \(V(Y) = E(Y^2) - [E(Y)]^2\text{:}\)
      \begin{equation*} V(Y) = 2.4 - (1.5)^2 = 2.4 - 2.25 = 0.15 \end{equation*}
    • \(V(Y) = 2.40\)
    • Incorrect. This is the value of \(E(Y^2)\) before subtracting the squared mean.
    • \(V(Y) = 0.25\)
    • Incorrect. Make sure to square the mean (\(1.5^2 = 2.25\)) when applying the variance formula subtraction step.
    • \(V(Y) = 0.90\)
    • Incorrect. Ensure you use the formula \(E(Y^2) - [E(Y)]^2\) instead of subtracting the raw mean value from \(E(Y^2)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    (d)
    Find the probability \(P(1 \le Y \le 2)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    • \(0.500\)
    • Incorrect. Remember that the distribution is skewed toward higher values because of the quadratic weight, so the upper half of the range contains a larger share of the probability.
    • \(0.125\)
    • Incorrect. This is the lower tail probability \(P(0 \le Y \le 1)\text{.}\) Subtract this value from 1 to find the correct upper tail area.
    • \(0.875\)
    • Correct! Integrating between the specified boundaries:
      \begin{equation*} P(1 \le Y \le 2) = \int_{1}^{2} \frac{3}{8}y^2 \, dy = \left[ \frac{y^3}{8} \right]_{1}^{2} = \frac{2^3}{8} - \frac{1^3}{8} = 1 - \frac{1}{8} = 0.875 \end{equation*}
    • \(0.750\)
    • Incorrect. Double-check your subtraction arithmetic when plugging the lower bound into your anti-derivative tool.
    🔗
    (e)
    Find the cumulative distribution function (CDF), \(F_Y(y)\text{,}\) for the interval \(0 \le y \le 2\text{.}\)
    🔗
    • \(F_Y(y) = \frac{y^3}{3}\)
    • Incorrect. You integrated \(y^2\) correctly to get \(\frac{y^3}{3}\text{,}\) but you forgot to multiply by the constant multiplier \(k = \frac{3}{8}\text{.}\)
    • \(F_Y(y) = \frac{y^3}{8}\)
    • Correct! To find the general cumulative distribution function, integrate the PDF from the lower limit 0 up to an arbitrary point \(y\text{:}\)
      \begin{equation*} F_Y(y) = \int_{0}^{y} \frac{3}{8}t^2 \, dt = \left[ \frac{t^3}{8} \right]_{0}^{y} = \frac{y^3}{8} \end{equation*}
      Evaluating this function at the boundaries confirms the probability from Part D: \(F_Y(2) - F_Y(1) = \frac{8}{8} - \frac{1}{8} = 0.875\text{.}\)
    • \(F_Y(y) = \frac{3y^3}{8}\)
    • Incorrect. It looks like you forgot to apply the power rule integration denominator (\(3\)) to cancel out the numerator coefficient when taking the anti-derivative.
    • \(F_Y(y) = \frac{y^2}{4}\)
    • Incorrect. Remember that integrating a function increases the polynomial power by 1 (\(y^2 \rightarrow y^3\)).
    🔗
    (f)
    Find the first quartile (\(Q_1\)) of this distribution. \(Q_1\) is the value below which exactly 25% of the probability mass lies.
    🔗
    • \(Q_1 = 0.750\)
    • Incorrect. This is the first quartile if the distribution were perfectly uniform over the domain. Because the quadratic curve squashes most of the area to the right, you must travel further down the axis to gather 25% of the total area.
    • \(Q_1 \approx 1.889\)
    • Correct! To find the first quartile, set the cumulative distribution function (CDF) derived in Part E equal to \(0.25\) (or \(\frac{1}{4}\)) and isolate \(y\text{:}\)
      \begin{equation*} F_Y(Q_1) = \frac{Q_1^3}{27} = \frac{1}{4} \implies Q_1^3 = \frac{27}{4} = 6.75 \end{equation*}
      Taking the cube root of both sides yields the final percentile threshold:
      \begin{equation*} Q_1 = \sqrt[3]{6.75} \approx 1.88948 \end{equation*}
      Rounded to three decimal places, the first quartile is approximately 1.889.
    • \(Q_1 \approx 1.442\)
    • Incorrect. It looks like you set the expression \(\frac{y^3}{3} = 1/4\text{,}\) forgetting to multiply by the baseline constant coefficient divisor of 9 in your integrated functional framework.
    • \(Q_1 \approx 2.250\)
    • Incorrect. This value matches the distribution mean \(E(Y)\) from Part B. For a highly left-skewed curve like this one, the mean is pulled right, so \(Q_1 \lt E(Y)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    (g)
    Calculate \(E(\sqrt{Y})\text{.}\)
    🔗
    • \(E(\sqrt{Y}) = 1.500\)
    • Incorrect. Jensen’s Inequality states that for a concave function like the square root, \(E(\sqrt{Y}) \le \sqrt{E(Y)}\text{.}\) Since \(\sqrt{2.25} = 1.5\text{,}\) your answer must be strictly less than 1.500. You cannot simply pull the expectation operator inside the radical.
    • \(E(\sqrt{Y}) \approx 1.469\)
    • Correct! By the Law of the Unconscious Statistician (LOTUS), to compute the expected value of a function \(g(y) = \sqrt{y}\text{,}\) we integrate \(g(y) \cdot f_Y(y)\) over the active domain boundaries:
      \begin{equation*} E(\sqrt{Y}) = \int_{0}^{3} \sqrt{y} \cdot \left(\frac{1}{9}y^2\right) \, dy = \frac{1}{9} \int_{0}^{3} y^{1/2} \cdot y^2 \, dy = \frac{1}{9} \int_{0}^{3} y^{5/2} \, dy \end{equation*}
      Applying the standard power integration rule adds 1 to the exponent (\(\frac{5}{2} + 1 = \frac{7}{2}\)) and multiplies by its inverse coefficient multiplier:
      \begin{equation*} = \frac{1}{9} \left[ \frac{2}{7}y^{7/2} \right]_{0}^{3} = \frac{2}{63} \left( 3^{7/2} - 0 \right) \end{equation*}
      Evaluating the fractional radical step:
      \begin{equation*} 3^{7/2} = 3^3 \cdot \sqrt{3} = 27\sqrt{3} \implies \frac{2 \cdot 27\sqrt{3}}{63} = \frac{54\sqrt{3}}{63} = \frac{6\sqrt{3}}{7} \approx 1.46899 \end{equation*}
      Rounded to three decimal places, the final functional expectation evaluates to approximately 1.469.
    • \(E(\sqrt{Y}) \approx 1.286\)
    • Incorrect. It looks like you evaluated the fraction \(\frac{27}{21}\) or dropped the trailing radical variable component during your anti-derivative conversion loop. Re-verify your index fractions.
    • \(E(\sqrt{Y}) \approx 1.714\)
    • Incorrect. Double-check your fractional division hierarchy steps. Remember that dividing by \(\frac{7}{2}\) requires multiplying your integrated bracket by its flipped parameter form, \(\frac{2}{7}\text{.}\)

    2. Continuous Distributions: Interpreting PDF Geometry.

    The graph below displays the probability density function (PDF), denoted as \(f_X(x)\text{,}\) for a random variable \(X\text{.}\) Use the graph (and your understanding of continuous random variables) to to answer the following questions.
    🔗
    🔗
    (a)
    Compare the probabilities at the two local features. Which is larger: \(P(X = 3)\) or \(P(X = 8)\text{?}\)
    🔗
    • \(P(X = 3)\)
    • Incorrect. Remember that for any continuous random variable, the probability of the variable equaling an exact single real-numbered point value is always zero, regardless of the height of the density graph at that coordinate.
    • \(P(X = 8)\)
    • Incorrect. While the spike around \(x = 8\) is much higher vertically, the probability at an exact single infinitely thin point coordinate is still zero.
    • They are about the same.
    • Correct! Because \(X\) is a continuous random variable, the probability at any single exact coordinate is defined mathematically by an integral over an interval of width zero. Therefore, \(P(X = 3) = 0\) and \(P(X = 8) = 0\text{,}\) making them exactly equal.
    🔗
    (b)
    Consider two tiny intervals of equal narrow width (\(\Delta x = 0.02\)). Which probability is larger: \(P(2.99 \le X \le 3.01)\) or \(P(7.99 \le X \le 8.01)\text{?}\)
    🔗
    • \(P(2.99 \le X \le 3.01)\)
    • Incorrect. The height of the density function determines the concentration of probability per unit width. Since the function is much lower at \(x=3\) than at the peak of the spike, a narrow interval here captures less area.
    • B. \(P(7.99 \le X \le 8.01)\)
    • Correct! For an extremely narrow window centered around a point \(c\text{,}\) the area under the curve can be approximated by a rectangle whose height is the density function value: \(P(c - \epsilon \le X \le c + \epsilon) \approx f_X(c) \cdot \Delta x\text{.}\) Reading our new shifted graph, the density height at the sharp peak is \(f_X(8) \approx 0.76\text{,}\) while the height of the plateau is \(f_X(3) = 0.15\text{.}\) Because the curve is much higher at \(x = 8\text{,}\) this tiny window traps significantly more area.
    • C. They are about the same.
    • Incorrect. Since the density heights at \(x=3\) and \(x=8\) are completely different, equal narrow intervals will contain different area allocations.
    🔗
    (c)
    Now look at wider intervals of equal width. Which probability is larger: \(P(1 \le X \le 3)\) or \(P(7 \le X \le 9)\text{?}\)
    🔗
    • \(P(1 \le X \le 3)\)
    • Correct! Probability corresponds to the total area under the density curve over the specified interval. Let us evaluate our new layout’s regions:
      • The interval \(1 \le X \le 3\) spans a width of 2 units over the plateau with a constant height of 0.15. This rectangular area evaluates to: \(2 \times 0.15 = 0.30\text{.}\)
      • The interval \(7 \le X \le 9\) captures the entire sharp spike centered at \(x = 8\text{.}\) Because this spike is extremely narrow, the entire feature contains only about \(0.125\) of total area.
      Because an area of \(0.30\) is significantly larger than \(0.125\text{,}\) the plateau interval on the left contains far more probability mass than the tall spike on the right.
    • \(P(7 \le X \le 9)\)
    • Incorrect. Do not let the maximum height of the spike mislead you. The spike at \(x=8\) is so narrow that it contains less total area than a 2-unit slice of our low, wide plateau structure.
    🔗
    (d)
    What is the approximate location of the median(\(m\)) for this random variable, \(X\text{?}\)
    🔗
    • \(m \approx 3.73\)
    • Correct! The median splits the entire area under the PDF into two equal halves of \(0.50\text{.}\) Because our initial plateau structure is wide (spanning 4 full units from \(x = 1\) to \(x = 5\)) and holds a constant height of \(0.15\text{,}\) the entire plateau contains a massive area block of \(4 \times 0.15 = 0.60\text{.}\) Since \(0.60 \gt 0.50\text{,}\) we reach the exact halfway area mark well before exiting the plateau. Solving for width \(w\) from the left edge: \(w \times 0.15 = 0.50 \implies w \approx 2.73\text{.}\) Adding this to our start point gives a median of exactly \(x \approx 3.73\).
    • \(m \approx 5.25\)
    • Incorrect. By the time you exit the plateau structure at \(x = 5.25\text{,}\) you have already accumulated roughly 63% of the total area under the curve, meaning the median point was already crossed further to the left.
    • \(m \approx 7.50\)
    • Incorrect. While the graph reaches its absolute highest peak mode at \(x = 7.50\text{,}\) the spike is too narrow to hold significant area mass. By the time you reach this spike, over 65% of the total probability space already sits behind you to the left.
    • \(m \approx 2.50\)
    • Incorrect. At the flat midpoint of the plateau (\(x = 2.50\)), the accumulated area from the left edge is only \(1.5 \times 0.15 = 0.225\text{,}\) which is far short of the required \(0.50\) barrier threshold.

    4. Reliability Engineering: Drone Battery Lifespans.

    The continuous operational lifespan before failure (measured in hundreds of hours) for a high-performance drone battery pack is modeled by a continuous random variable \(Y\) with the cumulative distribution function (CDF):
    \begin{equation*} F_Y(y) = 1 - e^{-y^2}, \quad y \le 0 \end{equation*}
    Complete the multiple-choice tasks below to analyze the reliability profile of these battery components.
    🔗
    (a)
    Find the probability that a randomly selected drone battery pack operates continuously for at least 200 hours.
    🔗
    • \(0.9817\)
    • Incorrect. This is the probability that the battery fails before 200 hours, \(P(Y \le 2) = F_Y(2)\text{.}\) You need the complement for "at least".
    • \(0.0183\)
    • Correct! Since the time unit is in hundreds of hours, 200 hours corresponds to \(y = 2\text{.}\)
      Using the complement rule:
      \begin{equation*} P(Y \ge 2) = 1 - F_Y(2) = 1 - (1 - e^{-2^2}) = e^{-4} \approx 0.0183 \end{equation*}
    • \(0.1353\)
    • Incorrect. It looks like you forgot to square the time parameter in the exponent, computing \(e^{-2}\) instead of \(e^{-4}\text{.}\)
    • \(0.3679\)
    • Incorrect. This is the probability of lasting at least 100 hours (\(y=1\)). Recompute using the target boundary for 200 hours.
    🔗
    (b)
    What is the probability that a drone battery pack operates for more than 100 hours given that it operates for at most 200 hours? In other words, evaluate the conditional probability: \(P(Y \gt 1 \mid Y \le 2)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    • \(0.3496\)
    • Incorrect. This is only the numerator value, which is the unconditional interval probability \(P(1 \lt Y \le 2)\text{.}\) You must divide this by the condition space \(P(Y \le 2)\text{.}\)
    • \(0.3561\)
    • Correct! Using the conditional probability identity:
      \begin{equation*} P(Y \gt 1 \mid Y \le 2) = \frac{P(1 \lt Y \le 2)}{P(Y \le 2)} = \frac{F_Y(2) - F_Y(1)}{F_Y(2)} \end{equation*}
      Evaluating the individual components:
      \begin{align*} F_Y(1) \amp = 1 - e^{-1} \approx 0.6321\\ F_Y(2) \amp = 1 - e^{-4} \approx 0.9817 \end{align*}
      Substituting back into the formula:
      \begin{equation*} \frac{0.9817 - 0.6321}{0.9817} = \frac{0.3496}{0.9817} \approx 0.3561 \approx 0.36 \end{equation*}
    • \(0.6321\)
    • Incorrect. This is the baseline probability of failure occurring within the first 100 hours, \(F_Y(1)\text{.}\)
    • \(0.5000\)
    • Incorrect. Review the conditional division rules for non-linear exponential distribution subsets.
    🔗
    (c)
    Which of the following expressions represents the mathematically valid probability density function (PDF), \(f_Y(y)\text{,}\) derived from the baseline battery model?
    🔗
    • \(f_Y(y) = e^{-y^2}, \quad y \le 0\)
    • Incorrect. This leaves out the necessary chain rule derivative multiplier when dealing with the variable inside the exponent.
    • \(f_Y(y) = 2ye^{-y^2}, \quad y \le 0\)
    • Correct! To obtain the probability density function \(f_Y(y)\) from the cumulative distribution function \(F_Y(y)\text{,}\) apply the first derivative with respect to \(y\) using the chain rule:
      \begin{equation*} f_Y(y) = \frac{d}{dy}[1 - e^{-y^2}] = 0 - e^{-y^2} \cdot \left(\frac{d}{dy}[-y^2]\right) = -e^{-y^2} \cdot (-2y) = 2ye^{-y^2} \end{equation*}
    • \(f_Y(y) = -2ye^{-y^2}, \quad y \le 0\)
    • Incorrect. A valid probability density function cannot evaluate to negative values over its valid domain support. Watch your signs when subtracting the internal derivative.
    • \(f_Y(y) = y^2 e^{-y^2}, \quad y \le 0\)
    • Incorrect. Remember that the power rule derivative of \(y^2\) yields \(2y\text{,}\) not \(y^2\text{.}\)

    5. Materials Engineering: Adhesive Curing Time Analysis.

    • \(\sigma_Y\)
    • \(0.34\)
    • \(0.15\)
    • \(6.0 \)

    🔗

    • The absolute minimum curing time required is \(\mu_Y\) hours, and the standard deviation indicates that all batches will finish curing by \(\mu_Y\)\(+\sigma_Y\) hours.
    • Incorrect. The support of the density function establishes that the minimum baseline curing time is 5.0 hours, and batches can legally take up to 7.0 hours to cure.
    • The network averages a \(\mu_Y\)% baseline optimization efficiency rate, while experiencing a strict \(\sigma_Y\) minute scheduling penalty per manufacturing shift.
    • Incorrect. This statement changes the units entirely and applies an out-of-context operational framework. Keep your interpretation grounded strictly in the hours required for chemical curing.

    6. Continuous Distributions: Differentiating CDFs and Linear Transformations.

    An archival geological dataset establishes that the random proportion \(Y\) of internal structural impurities per raw gemstone sample collected follows a continuous distribution with Cumulative Distribution Function (CDF) given by:
    \begin{equation*} F(y) = \frac{1}{2}y^3 + \frac{1}{2}y^2, 0 \lt y \lt 1 \end{equation*}
    🔗
